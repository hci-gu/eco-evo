# Seal diet, prey consumption and foraging in the Skagerrak-Kattegat (harbour seal; grey seal secondary)

Scope note: all diet percentages below carry their metric. Four metrics appear and are NOT
interchangeable: combined index Qi (Sorlie 2020), share of reconstructed biomass, share of
otolith counts (numbers), and share of ingested energy. A small fish (sandeel, Norway pout)
can dominate by number and be minor by mass. Own calculations appear only under "Inferences"
and are labelled "(own calc)". Model numbers used: seal biomass 15 t (~190 seals x 80 kg),
gadoid biomass 2500 t (start) or 9700 t (IBTS scale), herring 12000 t, gadoid 5.5 MJ/kg,
herring 4 MJ/kg lean (up to ~11 MJ/kg with fat), tick = 6 h, 1460 ticks/yr.

## 1. Diet composition of harbour seals in Swedish/Norwegian Skagerrak and the Kattegat (and grey seals in the Kattegat)

### Takeaway
Recent Skagerrak harbour seal diet is dominated by gadoids and flatfish (Norwegian Skagerrak:
gadoids + pleuronectids = 87.1 % of biomass; plaice alone 24.1 % of biomass; cod only 2.3 %),
with sandeel and Norway pout dominant by number in a 2020 sample; herring has fallen from a
major historical energy source (~24 % of energy) to a minor one (~3 % of energy in 2019 scats,
10 % by Qi in Norwegian Skagerrak). No quantitative Kattegat harbour seal diet and no grey seal
Kattegat diet was obtained.

### Cited Findings
**Norwegian Skagerrak (Telemark and Aust-Agder) - Sorlie, Nilssen, Bjorge & Freitas 2020, Marine Biology Research 16(4), doi 10.1080/17451000.2020.1751205. Method: otolith identification in scats.**
- 20 fish species/groups in the diet. — [MARMAM announcement of Sorlie et al. 2020](https://devlists.uvic.ca/archives/list/marmam@devlists.uvic.ca/thread/UPVIG5KGI64C4BII2GK42C3Z4MXDBSGB/)
- By combined index Qi: haddock/pollack/saithe 32.7 %, genus Trisopterus (Norway pout/poor cod/bib) 12.5 %, plaice 12.4 %, herring 10.0 %. — [MARMAM announcement](https://devlists.uvic.ca/archives/list/marmam@devlists.uvic.ca/thread/UPVIG5KGI64C4BII2GK42C3Z4MXDBSGB/)
- By biomass: plaice the largest single prey at 24.1 %. Gadoids + pleuronectids together 88.6 % (Qi) and 87.1 % (biomass). Cod 0.7 % (Qi) and 2.3 % (biomass). — [MARMAM announcement](https://devlists.uvic.ca/archives/list/marmam@devlists.uvic.ca/thread/UPVIG5KGI64C4BII2GK42C3Z4MXDBSGB/)
- Herring's biomass share in Sorlie 2020 was not available in the material reached (only its Qi of 10.0 %).

**Skagerrak (Kappa, Harkonen, Freitas, Carroll & Harding 2026, Marine Mammal Science 42(1): e70069, doi 10.1111/mms.70069; Crossref date Sept 2025). Method: otoliths in scats collected 2020, compared with historical datasets from 1977/78 and 1989.**
- 2020 diet contained 28 fish species. By otolith number: sandeel 39.6 %, Norway pout 21.8 %. By relative biomass: haddock/pollack/saithe 18.7 %, ballan wrasse 13.5 % (the two largest). — [Crossref abstract of Kappa et al. 2026](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/mms.70069)
- Compared with 1977/78 and 1989: "a notable decline in cod and herring consumption and an increase in sandeel and haddock/pollack/saithe"; authors question whether the current diet meets energetic needs given less energy-rich herring. — [Crossref abstract of Kappa et al. 2026](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/mms.70069)
- Secondary (popular-science summary, flagged): the historical samples were, by otolith number, Norway pout 34.7 %, sandeel 11.7 %, herring 10.3 %; 2020 blue whiting 7.9 % by number; samples described as 73 scats from three haul-outs in northern Skagerrak. — [Oceanbites summary](https://oceanbites.org/fishy-changes-to-seal-diet-over-time/). Note: the same blog writes "herring/pollack/saithe 12.8 %", which contradicts the paper's own "haddock/pollack/saithe" grouping; treat that blog figure as garbled. The blog also says 19 species where the abstract says 28.

**Northern Skagerrak, Swedish GU (University of Gothenburg) MSc thesis, 2019 scats. Method: otoliths; energy reconstruction compared with historical diets.**
- Herring's share of ingested energy fell from ~24 % (historical) to 2.8 % (2019). Flatfish made up 46 % of the energy in the recent diet vs 5.7-18 % historically. Overall energy content of the recent diet was intermediate relative to historical diets. — [GUPEA: Diet of the Skagerrak harbor seal: How has the energy content in the diet changed during the last decades?](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/cdff6716-c000-48f8-8a86-684cee6b2510) (abstract text via search snippets; the page itself was bot-blocked)
- The same thesis notes pup numbers in the northern Skagerrak population declining since 2012, with food limitation suggested. — [GUPEA thesis record](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/cdff6716-c000-48f8-8a86-684cee6b2510)
- Note the conflict: 2019 scats -> flatfish 46 % of energy (thesis) vs 2020 scats -> haddock/pollack/saithe and ballan wrasse top by biomass, sandeel/Norway pout top by number (Kappa 2026). Different years, possibly different haul-outs and different metrics; they cannot be merged into one table.

**Swedish west coast / Kattegat, qualitative (K. Harding, professor GU, Vara Rovdjur 2/2022):**
- Harbour seals hunt on open bottoms, not in the kelp belt or the free water mass; they are opportunistic and switch species when one declines. In the latest Skagerrak diet sample the commonest fish were blue whiting, Norway pout ("vitlinglyra"), poor cod ("glyskolja"), rockling ("albrosme") and sandeel. In the Kattegat they eat more flatfish, e.g. long rough dab ("lerskadda") and dab ("sandskadda"). — [Harding 2022, Vara Rovdjur (PDF)](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Energy-rich herring and cod have been replaced by lower-energy poor cod, Norway pout and rockling; an ongoing study points to prey size falling from ~10-30 cm to ~7-20 cm. — [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Swedish harbour seals eat about 30 fish species, including sprat, blue whiting and sandeel (news article, Univ. of Gothenburg research). — [Deep Sea Reporter](https://www.deepseareporter.com/?p=32682)

**Older Skagerrak-Kattegat work (citations only; contents not reached):**
- Harkonen T. 1987, "Seasonal and regional variations in the feeding habits of the harbour seal, Phoca vitulina, in the Skagerrak and the Kattegat", J. Zool. Lond. 213 (pages given as 1-9 or 535-543 in different catalogues). — [Otolith North Sea literature record](https://otoliths-northsea.linnaeus.naturalis.nl/linnaeus_ng/app/views/literature2/reference.php?id=39687)
- Harkonen 1988, harbour seal and cormorant food habits in Skagerrak/Kattegat; species list includes cod, plaice, flounder, dab. — [Otolith North Sea literature record](https://otoliths-northsea.linnaeus.naturalis.nl/linnaeus_ng/app/views/literature2/reference.php?id=39688)
- Baltic-region synthesis including Kattegat: >45,000 otoliths from 3147 scat and digestive-tract samples, 1968-2013, harbour, grey and ringed seals; large interspecific, spatial and seasonal variation; herring, sprat, cod and sandeel had high occurrence across species/areas. — [Scharff-Olsen et al. 2018, ICES JMS, Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/icesjms/fsy159)

**Grey seal:**
- Baltic fallback only: grey seal diet 2001-2004 (145 seals) dominated by herring by number and biomass, with whitefish and sprat also important. — [Lundstrom et al. 2007/2010, NAMMCO Sci. Publ. via search](https://doi.org/10.7557%2F3.2733)

### Inferences
- (own calc / judgement) For a model without a flatfish group, the Skagerrak harbour seal is overwhelmingly a demersal-fish eater: on biomass, roughly 85-90 % of the diet is gadoid or flatfish in Norwegian Skagerrak (Sorlie), and the energy-based Swedish thesis gives flatfish alone 46 % of energy. Herring is a minor item today (2.8 % of energy, 10 % by Qi).
- The 2020 sample (Kappa) adds two large prey that are neither gadoid nor flatfish: sandeel (dominant by number) and ballan wrasse (13.5 % by biomass). Diet varies strongly between years and sites, so any single split is uncertain by tens of percentage points.

### Gaps
- No quantitative harbour seal diet for the Swedish or Danish Kattegat was obtained (only Harding's qualitative "more flatfish"); Harkonen 1987/1988 contents not accessible.
- Grey seal diet in the Kattegat: nothing found.
- Sorlie 2020 sampling years, months, number of scats and seasonal split not obtained (full text was paywall/bot-blocked).
- Seasonal variation in Skagerrak diet not quantified in anything reached.

## 2. Annual consumption by seals in Skagerrak-Kattegat, and as a fraction of stock biomass or natural mortality

### Takeaway
The only area-specific tonnage found is Sorlie 2020: ~315 t of fish per year for the Telemark/Aust-Agder harbour seals, of which 7.1 t cod (~5 % of local cod landings). The classic per-seal ration is 4680 kcal/day ingested (~19.6 MJ/day), equal to ~4.1 kg flatfish/day (Harkonen & Heide-Jorgensen 1991, Skagerrak). No Skagerrak-Kattegat estimate of seal predation as a fraction of gadoid or herring stock biomass or natural mortality was found.

### Cited Findings
- Sorlie et al. 2020: estimated total consumption ~315 t/yr, dominated by non-commercial species; cod 7.1 t/yr, ~5 % of annual cod landings; competition with local fisheries judged limited. — [MARMAM announcement](https://devlists.uvic.ca/archives/list/marmam@devlists.uvic.ca/thread/UPVIG5KGI64C4BII2GK42C3Z4MXDBSGB/)
- Harkonen & Heide-Jorgensen (1991), as cited by Aarts et al. 2018: harbour seals in the Skagerrak (Sweden) have, averaged over the population structure, an ingested energy requirement of 4680 kcal/day (adult males 5890 kcal/day); this needs ~4.1 kg flatfish per day. Captive ad-libitum harbour seals: 6071 kcal/day (Rosen & Renouf 1998). — [Aarts et al. 2018, bioRxiv 10.1101/267567](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/267567.full.pdf). Citation note: the Ophelia 34(3):191-207 (1991) paper "The harbour seal Phoca vitulina as a predator in the Skagerrak" is listed under Harkonen alone in [marineinfo/IMIS](https://marineinfo.org/doc/publication/17893); the two-author attribution comes from Aarts et al. Not resolved.
- Wadden Sea fallback (Aarts et al. 2018): with a diet averaging 1.007 kcal/g the same 4680 kcal requirement gives ~4.6 kg/day per seal; 12,000 seals -> ~20,400 t/yr; seal predation estimated at an average annual mortality of 43 % (Wadden Sea) and 60 % (adjacent coastal zone) of local prey fish biomass; flounder 39 % of diet by fresh weight. — [Aarts et al. 2018](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/267567.full.pdf)
- Same source, citing North Sea grey seal work: grey seal predation could explain 20-50 % of natural mortality of some demersal fish species even when they are a small part of the diet. — [Aarts et al. 2018](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/267567.full.pdf)
- Population size context: about 22,000 harbour seals along the Swedish west coast down to Oresund (news report); ~25,000 in Kattegat + Skagerrak + Kalmarsund across Sweden, Norway and Denmark (Harding 2022). — [Deep Sea Reporter](https://www.deepseareporter.com/?p=32682); [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Popular estimates (not peer-reviewed, flagged): a hunter quoted on TV4 computes 25,000 seals x 4 kg/day = 100 t/day along the west coast; SLU's S.-G. Lunneryd argued the population (25-30k) should fall to ~10,000 for cod recovery. — [TV4](https://www.tv4.se/artikel/EPuN9xuqgvX952skcY6Xx/jakten-pa-knubbsael-behoever-oeka-foer-att-raedda-haven). Harding (2022) counters that no research supports a measurable positive effect on fish stocks from reducing harbour seals. — [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Hansson et al. 2018 ("Competition for the fish") covers the Baltic only (not Skagerrak-Kattegat): seals and birds likely have limited impact on herring, sprat and cod stocks, but consume 2-3 times as much coastal fish as the fishery catches. — [SLU press release](https://slu.se/en/ew-news/2017/11/seals-birds-and-humans-compete-for-fish-in-the-baltic-sea); [marine.gov.scot record](https://marine.gov.scot/sma/node/18057)

### Inferences
- (own calc) 4680 kcal/day = 19.6 MJ/day ingested. 4.1 kg flatfish/day implies ~4.8 MJ/kg prey. For an 80 kg seal 4.1 kg/day is 5.1 % of body mass per day = 0.0128 t prey / t seal / tick at 4 ticks/day.
- (own calc) Paid in the model's gadoid (5.5 MJ/kg): 19.6/5.5 = 3.6 kg/day. Paid in herring: 4.9 kg/day at 4 MJ/kg (lean), 2.8 kg/day at 7 MJ/kg, 1.8 kg/day at 11 MJ/kg.
- (own calc) Model grid ration: 15 t seals (~190 animals) x 4.1 kg/80 kg x 365 = ~280 t/yr (mass-based), or ~245 t/yr if the 19.6 MJ/day is paid entirely in 5.5 MJ/kg gadoid. This is the same order as Sorlie's 315 t/yr for the Telemark/Aust-Agder coast (seal count behind that number not obtained).
- (own calc) Herring pair: a literature herring share of 3-10 % of the ration gives 8-28 t/yr, i.e. predation_mortality ~0.0007-0.0023 /yr on 12,000 t. The current 0.0076 /yr implies 91 t/yr = ~33 % of the seals' whole ration, which no diet study here supports (herring 2.8 % energy in 2019, 10 % Qi in Sorlie). This is the clearest mismatch.
- (own calc) Gadoid pair (with flatfish lumped in): at an 85-95 % diet share (230-265 t/yr on the mass-based ration), the rate is ~0.09-0.11 /yr if gadoid biomass is 2500 t and ~0.023-0.028 /yr if it is 9700 t (IBTS). The current 0.0299 /yr is consistent with the 9700 t biomass; at 2500 t it covers only ~75 t/yr, ~27 % of the ration. The rate and the gadoid biomass it is divided by are coupled; whichever is changed, the other must follow.
- (own calc) Note that the current pair rates (0.0299 x 9700 t + 0.0076 x 12,000 t = ~380 t/yr) would exceed the ~280 t/yr ration of 15 t of seals.

### Gaps
- No ICES WGSAM/SMS, SLU Aqua or DTU Aqua estimate of seal consumption of cod/gadoids/herring for Skagerrak-Kattegat, nor of seal-induced M2 as a share of M, was found (searches returned Baltic-only Hansson 2018 and a porpoise-on-Kattegat-cod thesis).
- The population count and daily ration assumption behind Sorlie's 315 t/yr were not obtained.
- Harkonen 1991 (Ophelia) consumption estimates for the whole Skagerrak were not accessible.

## 3. What seals eat that the model lacks, and the gadoid:herring split if flatfish are lumped into gadoids

### Takeaway
Non-model prey in the Skagerrak diet are mainly flatfish (plaice, dab, long rough dab), sandeel, wrasse (ballan wrasse), rocklings and blue whiting (gobies were not quantified in any source reached). With flatfish and small demersal fish lumped into gadoids, the model split is roughly 85-95 % gadoids : 3-10 % pelagic_fish (herring), depending on study and normalisation.

### Cited Findings
- Gadoids + pleuronectids = 87.1 % of biomass (Sorlie 2020); herring 10.0 % by Qi; cod 2.3 % of biomass. — [MARMAM announcement](https://devlists.uvic.ca/archives/list/marmam@devlists.uvic.ca/thread/UPVIG5KGI64C4BII2GK42C3Z4MXDBSGB/)
- 2020 sample: sandeel 39.6 % and Norway pout 21.8 % of otoliths; haddock/pollack/saithe 18.7 % and ballan wrasse 13.5 % of biomass. — [Kappa et al. 2026 abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/mms.70069)
- 2019 sample: flatfish 46 % of energy; herring 2.8 % of energy. — [GUPEA thesis](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/cdff6716-c000-48f8-8a86-684cee6b2510)
- Blue whiting, Norway pout, poor cod, rockling and sandeel the commonest fish in the latest Skagerrak sample; more flatfish (long rough dab, dab) in the Kattegat. — [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)

### Inferences
- (own calc, normalisation A: drop non-model prey and renormalise) Sorlie biomass: gadoids+flatfish 87.1 %, leaving <= 12.9 % for herring and everything else; herring's Qi is 10 %, so herring is somewhere between ~3 % and ~10 % of what remains -> split roughly 90:10 to 97:3 gadoids:pelagic_fish. GU thesis energy: herring 2.8 %, flatfish 46 %, rest mostly gadoids -> ~97:3.
- (own calc, normalisation B: lump all small benthic/demersal non-model fish - flatfish, wrasse, rockling, gobies - into gadoids, and sandeel into pelagic_fish) Using the 2020 sample, sandeel's large numeric share shrinks a lot by mass (sandeel are small); without its biomass share, the pelagic_fish share is plausibly 5-20 % of mass. Sandeel is a burrowing, schooling forage fish of energy ~4-6 MJ/kg; mapping it to pelagic_fish is a modelling choice, not a literature statement.
- (judgement) Recommended working split for seals -> {gadoids, pelagic_fish}: ~90:10 by mass, with the herring share closer to 3-5 % if only herring/sprat are counted as pelagic_fish (2019-2020 data) and ~10-25 % only if historical (1970s-80s) diets are the target.
- Lumping flatfish into gadoids inflates the gadoid share relative to "true" gadoid consumption: cod is only 2.3 % of biomass, and haddock/pollack/saithe + Trisopterus are mostly small juveniles.

### Gaps
- Sandeel and herring biomass shares in the 2020 sample (Kappa) were not available; only otolith-number and top-biomass values were.
- Gobies: no Skagerrak share found.

## 4. Prey size selection, foraging depth (benthic vs pelagic), day/night foraging

### Takeaway
Skagerrak harbour seals take mostly small fish, below minimum landing size (~10-30 cm historically, now ~7-20 cm), hunting on open soft bottoms rather than in the water column; dives to a couple of hundred metres are possible. No day/night data for the region were found.

### Cited Findings
- Seals generally prefer small fish below minimum landing size (Norwegian Skagerrak). — [MARMAM announcement of Sorlie 2020](https://devlists.uvic.ca/archives/list/marmam@devlists.uvic.ca/thread/UPVIG5KGI64C4BII2GK42C3Z4MXDBSGB/)
- Most fish in diet studies are 10-30 cm; seals do not take large cod > 40 cm or fast mackerel; an ongoing study suggests prey size has fallen to ~7-20 cm. Harbour seals hunt on open bottoms, not in the kelp belt or free water mass. — [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Seals mainly eat fish 7-30 cm; can dive to a couple of hundred metres. — [Deep Sea Reporter](https://www.deepseareporter.com/?p=32682)
- Fallback (UK): seals foraging in deeper water over sandy substrate show more pelagic/bentho-pelagic prey such as blue whiting and sandeel. — [PLoS ONE 2019 via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6314570)
- Fallback (Wadden Sea): harbour seals spend ~17 % of their time hauled out. — [Aarts et al. 2018](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/267567.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- (judgement) For the model, seal predation acts mainly on the demersal (bottom-associated) part of the gadoid group and on small/juvenile fish; herring are taken when near the bottom or in winter aggregations. Size selection means seal M2 falls on young age classes, not spawners (Harding argues this lowers the impact on stocks).

### Gaps
- No Skagerrak-Kattegat dive-depth distribution or day/night foraging data were found; any diel attack-rate pattern for seals would be unsupported by local data.

## 5. Functional response and prey switching (Type II vs III; handling time, half-saturation)

### Takeaway
All pinniped studies found support a Type III (sigmoidal) functional response with prey switching: grey seals in the UK (Smout et al. 2014, multispecies FR), harbour seals and salmonids in Scotland (Middlemas et al. 2006), and grey seal, harbour seal and porpoise in the North Sea (2023 St Andrews PhD). No handling-time or half-saturation values were obtained. Skagerrak diet shifts (herring/cod down, sandeel/gadoids up) and Harding's "they switch when a species declines" are qualitative local support.

### Cited Findings
- Smout, Rindorf, Hammond, Harwood & Matthiopoulos (ICES JMS 71: 81-89, published online 2013): a Multi-Species Functional Response fitted (Bayesian) to UK grey seal diet and prey availability; "Both hyperbolic (Type 2) and sigmoidal (Type 3) functional response models were fitted ... the Type 3 model was clearly favoured during model selection", implying switching and possible predator-pit effects for some prey. — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/icesjms/fst109); [Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/90470)
- Middlemas, Barton, Armstrong & Thompson (Proc. R. Soc. B 273: 193-198, 2006): harbour seal numbers in a Scottish river tracked returning salmon abundance (aggregative response), and dietary data supported a Type III functional response. — [PMC1560021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1560021/)
- 2023 St Andrews PhD (Smout supervisor): grey seals, harbour seals and harbour porpoise in the North Sea all show Type III functional responses; sandeel more strongly preferred by grey seals and porpoise than by harbour seals. — [St Andrews research portal](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/marine-mammal-predator-prey-interactions-in-the-north-sea/)
- Harbour seals are opportunistic and switch to other species when one declines ("do not hunt the last cod"). — [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Kappa et al. interpret the 1977/89-2020 diet change as dietary adaptation to available prey. — [Kappa et al. 2026 abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/mms.70069)

### Inferences
- (own judgement) For the model, both seal pairs (seals->gadoids, seals->pelagic_fish) are better forced to Type III (`functional_response: 3`) than left at Type II; seals are generalists, which under the model's default rule would give Type II, so this needs the explicit override.
- (own calc) Handling-time ceiling check: the mean ration is 0.0128 t/t/tick vs the model ceiling 1/h = 0.05 t/t/tick, so seals need ~26 % of the ceiling on average; a ceiling of ~4x mean ration (~20 % of body mass/day) is not contradicted by anything found and allows for feeding on only part of the ticks (seals haul out ~17 % of the time in the Wadden Sea). No change is implied by the literature.

### Gaps
- No published handling time, maximum consumption or half-saturation density for harbour or grey seals was obtained (Smout 2014 parameter values are in the paywalled full text).
- No functional response estimate from Skagerrak-Kattegat itself.

## 6. Foraging trip range and duration from haul-outs; use of waters 10-60 km offshore

### Takeaway
Harbour seals in the Skagerrak-Kattegat are mostly local (haul-outs mean ~16 km from tagging site; "seldom beyond 50 km"; almost all Anholt at-sea positions within 40 km), but individuals range 100-260 km and move between Norway, Sweden and Denmark. Waters 10-60 km offshore are within normal foraging range near colonies, but seal density should fall off with distance from haul-outs.

### Cited Findings
- Riaz et al. 2026 (Ecology and Evolution, doi 10.1002/ece3.72718): 28 harbour seals captured (26 with usable GPS phone-tag haul-out data) in the post-moult period (Aug-Nov) 2017-2022 at Askeroy (Aust-Agder), Jomfruland (Telemark), Bolaerne (Vestfold) and Hvaler (Ostfold). Haul-outs typically within 25 km of the tagging site (mean 16.2 km, 95 % CI 6.7-25.7 km); range 0-260 km; one Aust-Agder seal reached Danish haul-outs > 260 km away; 54 % made repeated cross-boundary movements; a strong hub at Hvaler/Koster (Norway-Sweden border); harbour seals "seldom moving beyond 50 km from natal haul-out/breeding sites" (citing Dietz et al.). — [Europe PMC full text PMC12796850](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12796850/fullTextXML); [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12796850/)
- Dietz et al. 2013 (ICES JMS): 27 harbour seals satellite-tagged at Anholt (central Kattegat) moved widely across the Kattegat, max 249 km from the tagging site; home ranges and travel distances peaked Feb-March; seals very stationary around Anholt in the spring breeding season; females and yearlings ranged more widely. — [search summary of Dietz et al. 2013](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/da/publications/movements-and-site-fidelity-of-harbour-seals-phoca-vitulina-in-ka/)
- Andersen et al. 2013 (Aquatic Conservation): 8 Anholt seals tagged spring 2008; almost all at-sea locations within 40 km of the haul-out; trip extent strongly tied to trip duration. — [openpolar record](https://openpolar.no/Record/crwiley:10.1002%2Faqc.2393)
- Seals tagged in the Oslofjord swim to the Kattegat and back within a few weeks (Harding). — [Harding 2022](https://goteborg.naturskyddsforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/sites/94/2024/09/Presentation-Karin-Harding-knubbsalar.pdf)
- Fallback (Scotland): 50 % of trips within 25 km of a haul-out, some > 100 km. — [search summary of Sharples et al. 2012, PMC3357409](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3357409/)
- Fallback (Wadden Sea): seals forage offshore up to and beyond 50 km, spending only ~13 % of diving time in the Wadden Sea itself; foraging rates during transit comparable to offshore rates (Vance et al. 2021). — [Aarts et al. 2018](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/267567.full.pdf); [Vance et al. 2021, Sci. Rep.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-021-85376-2)

### Inferences
- (own judgement) The model grid (57.81-58.31 N, 10.07-11.15 E) has its eastern edge near the Bohuslan coast, i.e. within ~25-50 km of major Swedish colonies (Koster/Vaderoarna region, the Hvaler/Koster hub in Riaz 2026). Seals using the grid's eastern half is consistent with the telemetry; the western, most offshore part (> 50 km from haul-outs) should see only occasional use. The grid's 15 t (~190 seals) is best read as a low-density offshore tail of a ~22,000-25,000-animal regional population, not as a colony.
- (own judgement) Because the grid contains no haul-outs, seals in the model forage continuously; real seals spend ~17 % of time hauled out, which is another reason not to raise the intake ceiling.
