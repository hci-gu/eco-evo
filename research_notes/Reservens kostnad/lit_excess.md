# Lean spring, fat autumn: acquisition, spawning or choice? Excess-food evidence

Question: field fish in the Skagerrak / North Sea are lean in spring and fat in autumn
(reserve fill: benthivores ~0.33 May-Jul vs 0.81 Sep-Nov; whiting ~0.30 vs 0.73; herring
0.20 Apr vs 0.96 Aug; mackerel 0.13 on arrival in May, 1.0 Sep-Oct). Is the spring low
(A) acquisition (intake fell), (B) spawning, or (C) a chosen, regulated, below-maximum
reserve? Discriminator: fish held on EXCESS food. A cycle in IMMATURE fish under excess
food = choice / clock; no cycle under excess food = acquisition; a cycle only in maturing
fish = spawning.

Tags: [F] full text read, [A] abstract only, [S] secondary (incl. search snippets and
material already covered), [D] derived here, [J] judgement. One row per number in
`tables/excess_raw.csv`. Downloads in `data/<source>/`, read only via `scripts/`.

Note on temperature: none of the excess-food studies found ran at CONSTANT temperature;
all are ambient (natural) temperature. So "no cycle under excess food" below means no
cycle under excess food at a natural temperature and (except where stated) natural
photoperiod or continuous light.

Already covered (not re-searched, cited [S]): salmonid lipostat (Johansen et al. 2001;
Jobling & Johansen 1999; Shearer et al. 1997; Metcalfe & Thorpe 1992; Bull 1996; Tveiten
1996; Saether 1996); herring photoperiod condition (Berg, Seljestad & Folkvord 2024; dos
Santos Schmidt et al. 2022); saithe at fish farms (Dempster et al. 2011; Uglem et al.
2020); farmed cod HSI > 12 % vs wild 2-6 %; cod livers 9-17 % HSI at 1.0-1.4 Cmax (Lekva;
Rosenlund 2004); spawning fasts in cod / haddock / plaice (Fordham & Trippel; Homans &
Vladykov; Rijnsdorp).

## 1. Captive fish on excess food (>= 1 year where available)

### Cod - Davie (2005) PhD thesis, Univ. Stirling (hdl 1893/109) [F]
File: data/davie2005_thesis/. Farmed cod, Machrihanish (Scotland), tanks, ambient
seawater 6-15 C (Fig 4.2, printed p.143), "fed a commercial dry pellet ... to satiation
3-5 times per day ... 7 days a week" (printed p.144, sec 4.3.1). Exp IV: 8 photoperiod
regimes, SNP (simulated natural) vs continuous light (LL) switched on at 6-18 months
post hatch; LL suppressed maturation.

- HSI, sec 4.4.3.1 (printed p.206): "Population means ranged from 3 to 10% ... Significant
  differences were only recorded in HSI in the months of April and July 2003 (24 and 27
  MPH). In April the HSI of treatments 1 and 7 fell to below 5%". The ANOVA compares
  treatments within a date, not dates within a treatment.
- Fig 4.31 (printed p.207), rendered and read by eye [D, +-0.5 HSI points; n = 4-11 per
  point, 3-monthly, Oct 2001 - Jul 2003]. All groups start at ~7.3 % (Oct 2001); Jan 2002
  ~8.4 (SNP) and ~10.0 (LL from Oct 2001); Apr 2002 ~8.4-9.0; Jul 2002 ~7.7-9.4; Oct 2002
  ~7.5-8.7. Juveniles under NATURAL photoperiod and satiation (groups 1, 5, 6 were all
  still on SNP until Jul / Oct 2002) therefore ran ~7.3 -> 8.4-8.7 (Jan-Apr 2002) -> ~7.5
  (Jul-Oct 2002): no spring low; at most a ~1-point (~12 %) summer-autumn dip. Second
  year: non-maturing LL groups 3, 5, 6 ~8.7-9.3 in Apr 2003 and ~7.2-8.8 in Jul 2003;
  maturing SNP groups 1, 7 ~6.4 (Jan 2003) -> 4.4-4.8 (Apr 2003) -> 5.6-6 (Jul 2003); group
  8 (back to SNP Jan 2003) 3.4 in Jul 2003.
- Condition, sec 4.4.2.6 (printed p.194): "Photoperiod 1 steadily increased in condition
  over the first 9 months of the trial from October 2001 to July 2002 (6 to 15 MPH)" -
  juveniles under natural light with satiation feeding gained condition through winter
  and spring.
- Sec 4.4.3.2 (printed p.206-208): pre-maturation SNP fish Jan 2002 "mean HSI 8.4%";
  spawning SNP fish Apr 2003 "reduced liver size (mean HSI of 4.4%)"; same-age immature
  LL fish Apr 2003 "classified as immature and had a similar HSI status (mean HSI of
  9.3%)". Liver lipid ~65 % in both immature groups vs ~40 % in the spawning group.
- Discussion (printed p.226): "the only significant difference noted was the temporary
  decline in HSI in populations 1, 7 and 8 which coincided with the peak in spawning
  activity ... The lack of detectable fluctuations in the other populations confirms ...
  the lack of maturation commitment".
- Exp I (sec 3.4, printed p.81-84): Fulton K cycled in both SNP and LL (SNP peak 1.45 Mar
  2001, 1.67 Feb 2002; LL peak 1.39 Apr 2001, 1.47 Apr 2002, low 1.23 Aug 2001). This is
  NOT a clean immature result: K is on whole wet weight and the LL fish showed a gonadal
  cycle by ultrasound; the author attributes the Apr-Aug drop to maturation (printed
  p.132). Printed p.132: farmed condition peaks Feb-Apr, "in conflict with ... Love
  (1970) who ... concluded condition factor peaks in cod to be from September to
  December. All studies discussed ... were based on wild caught ... fish. This highlights
  the difference ... between cultured fish that have feed available all year round in
  comparison to wild origin fish that have exogenous restrictions like seasonal food
  availability."
- Refill (sec 3.6.2, printed p.128-129): SNP spawning weight losses 11 and 19 %; after
  spawning ended in July the SNP fish returned "to statistically comparable weights with
  the LL population within a month. Within 3 months both populations displayed
  comparable weight and growth rates".

### Cod - Karlsen et al. (2006) ICES JMS 63:355, doi 10.1016/j.icesjms.2005.10.013 [F]
Full text read through a fetch summary that returned verbatim sentences (no local copy;
OUP blocks direct download). Juveniles hatched spring 1996, tanks Jul 1997 - Aug 1998,
"fed to satiation" daily (19.5 MJ/kg dry feed, 10 % fat), ambient water from 58 m,
~6-10 C. LL fish: "no maturing or mature fish were observed in the LL groups throughout
the study."
- HSI 11.5 % at start (June 1997), 10-12 % until Jan/Feb 1998; reduced in most groups in
  August 1997; April 1998 "NL groups had HSIs of 4-7%, and LL groups had HSIs of ca.
  11%" (males); August 1998 "HSIs ranged from 7% to 10% in fish of both sexes and in all
  groups." Discussion: "liver indices were restored to prespawning levels at the end of
  the experiment in August 1998".
- NL had higher HSI than LL in Aug-Nov 1997, before any spawning: a light effect on the
  liver of pre-maturing fish ("The NL groups had significantly higher HSI than the LL
  groups"; gone by January).
- Temperature: ~7 C (Jul 1997) rising to ~10 C (Nov 1997), ~6 C (Apr 1998), ~9 C (Aug
  1998) - August is NOT the warmest month here.
- Reading: non-maturing (LL) cod under satiation hold 10-12 % for most of the year, then
  fall to 7-10 % by August 1998 (up to a third of the plateau). This summer decline over a
  13-month series is unexplained (not temperature-timed); it has the opposite phase to the
  wild spring low. Maturing fish drop to 4-7 % at spawning and are back to prespawning
  levels at the next sample (August; i.e. within <= 4 months - a sampling-interval bound).
- Caveat for both cod studies: the immature fish are mostly under CONTINUOUS LIGHT, which
  removes the cue a photoperiod-entrained set point would use. They rule out a free-running
  or temperature-driven spring low, not a light-entrained one. Davie's groups 1, 5, 6 in
  their first year (natural light, satiation, not yet maturing) are the cleaner test, and
  they show no spring low either.

### Cod - Mullowney & Rose (2014) ICES JMS 71:784, doi 10.1093/icesjms/fst188 [F]
(fetch summary; no numbers in text). Wild-caught cod (~48 cm, mean age 4) held 472-550 d,
fed to satiation (capelin, squid, herring, mackerel, shrimp) twice weekly, ambient
2-12 C. Condition followed "a general pattern of peaking prior to spawning, declining
through the spawning period, and increasing thereafter"; captive fish from both areas
reached equivalent prime condition, wild fish did not (abstract: lack of capelin).

### Haddock
- Hislop, Ross & Gauld (1978) J Fish Biol 13:85, doi 10.1111/j.1095-8649.1978.tb03416.x
  [A]: individual captive haddock on graded rations Nov to end of spawning; egg
  production correlated with feeding level; G = 0.295 E - 0.328. Not a full year, no
  liver data in abstract.
- Farmed juvenile haddock HSI 10.0-15.2 % rising with dietary lipid 12 -> 24 % (Nanton
  et al. 2003, CBP-B, doi 10.1016/s1096-4959(03)00052-6; temperature not stated) and
  industry HSI 14 % at 200 g, 17 % at 400 g [S, search snippets]. No seasonal series
  found.

### Herring - Atlantic herring: Berg, Seljestad & Folkvord (2024) [S, already covered]
From research_notes/"Ljusstyrd aptit"/summary.md (not re-searched): herring held 3.5 years
at a CONSTANT 7 or 10 C with food "in excess", natural vs 6-month offset light. Condition
(weight-at-length residual) swung 3.6-8.3 % of weight, minimum in winter, maximum late
summer-autumn, phase following the light in 3 of 4 groups; first winter immature. That
note's fill conversion: 0.04-0.66 fill; its open phase gap: lab minimum at the winter
solstice vs field minimum in April. This is the only target-species evidence of a
light-entrained seasonal state under excess food.

### Herring - PROXY Pacific herring: Sewall, Norcross & Heintz (2021) CJFAS 78:881,
doi 10.1139/cjfas-2020-0293 [F] (data/cjfas2021_pacherring/)
YOY Clupea pallasii, ambient 3.7-7.7 C, natural winter photoperiod, Nov 2011 - May 2012
(20 weeks, not a year), full ration = "fed daily to satiation" (p.882).
- Table 2 (p.885), full ration, wet lipid %: Jan 7.56 +- 2.20, Apr 8.72 +- 1.80, May
  9.34 +- 2.74 (ns). Reduced ration (Table 3): 7.56, 7.73, 6.49. No winter lipid decline
  under excess food.
- But growth was minimal and gut mass fell even in fully fed fish (p.891): "gut atrophy
  among fish provided excess food suggests influence by other environmental cues such as
  limited daylight hours or low temperatures." Authors' interpretation (not data):
  "An overwintering strategy of metabolically consuming stored fat thus may be preferred
  to foraging ... regardless of zooplankton prey availability" (p.890-891); full-fed fish
  "were not allocating all available energy consumed towards growth" (p.891).
- Caveat: heavy disease mortality (25 % winter, 36 % spring in full ration; VHS/VEN), and
  it was size-selective (dead fish 3.5-7 mm shorter, Table 9, p.890): the survivors' lipid
  trend may partly be selection. Weak proxy: 20 weeks, other species.

## 2. Field: condition vs intake

- Cod, southern Gulf of St Lawrence: Schwalme & Chouinard (1999) ICES JMS 56:303, doi
  10.1006/jmsc.1999.0458 [A]: "Cod fed very little during the five winter months
  (December to April) and lost 25-27% of their carcass weight and 76-84% of their liver
  dry weight"; "81-96% of the loss ... over winter was used to meet metabolic energy
  requirements" (gonads 4-19 %). Immature fish not separated in the abstract. This is an
  intake fall; whether from prey shortage or cold-season feeding cessation is not
  separable from the abstract.
- Cod, Placentia Bay: Mello & Rose (2005) ICES JMS, doi 10.1016/j.icesjms.2005.03.008 [A]:
  K and HSI lowest in spring (spawning), maximum by fall; HSI +82 %, K +24 % spring to
  fall.
- Cod, northern Gulf: Dutil, Lambert & Chabot (ICES CM 2000, figshare 25636833) [S,
  snippet]: decline in wild condition Apr-May matched the lab starvation rate.
- Cod, Lambert & Dutil (2000) CJFAS 57:815, doi 10.1139/f00-022 [A]: "The condition of
  spent females suggested a greater impact of changes in environmental conditions on
  adult than on immature cod."
- Whiting, North Sea: Lauerburg et al. (2018) MEPS 594:213, doi 10.3354/meps12533 [F]
  (data/lauerburg2018_whiting/). Table 3 (p.222): female median HSI 30-34.9 cm 2.88 (2007)
  vs 3.72 (2012); 35-39.9 cm 2.58 vs 4.36; 40-49.9 cm 2.80 vs 6.10; but 25-29.9 cm (the
  class nearest to immature) the other way, 3.76 (2007) vs 3.13 (2012), p < 0.001. 2007 = poor forage
  fish, sampled 1.5 months after spawning; 2012 = good forage fish, mid-spawning. p.226:
  "the energy stores were severely depleted, as indicated by the low HSI values. The
  samples in 2007 were taken 1.5 mo after the end of the spawning season ... when HSI
  indices should have recovered". Abstract: stomach content mass "60% less" in the poor
  period. Whiting liver level of fish >= 30 cm tracks prey supply; the small-fish class
  does not, so the immature response is unresolved.
- Six North Sea species: Pedersen & Hislop (2001) J Fish Biol 59:380, doi
  10.1111/j.1095-8649.2001.tb00137.x [A] (saithe, haddock, whiting, Norway pout, herring,
  sprat): "Seasonal differences in ED were obvious in mature fish, while geographical
  differences were insignificant." Full text not reachable; whether immature fish showed
  any cycle is not stated in the abstract.
- Norway pout: Nielsen et al. (2012) ICES JMS 69:197, doi 10.1093/icesjms/fss001 [A]:
  mortality concentrated Q1 -> Q2, correlated with maturity, sex, growth - spawning
  stress.
- Hake: Lloret, Demestre & Sanchez-Pardo (2008) Vie et Milieu 58(1) [S, snippet]:
  pre-spawning females had lower liver lipid than juveniles and maturing fish (single
  June survey, NW Mediterranean).
- Mackerel: Jansen et al. (2021) Prog Oceanogr 198:102658, doi
  10.1016/j.pocean.2021.102658 [F] (data/jansen2021_mackerel/). Sec 3.1: lipid "changed
  radically within the year, i.e., up to 25 percent points", decreasing -0.021 %/day in
  Sep-Oct. Sec 3.3: 98 % "resting" (stage 8) mid-Aug to Nov; "Growth in weight and length
  ceased in late September ... we assumed that all energy was used for either
  respiration (including swimming) or gonad development from the first of October" (an
  assumption of the authors, not an observation of zero feeding).
  Sec 3.2: "mackerel feed intensively during the spawning season (mainly after March) ...
  do not finish spawning before starting to feed". Adults only.
- Herring PROXY field: age-0 Pacific herring PWS whole-body energy 4.4 (Oct), 5.0 (Nov),
  3.9 kJ/g (Mar) as zooplankton declined; feeding did not meet metabolic need (Foy & Paul
  1999) [S, snippet].

## 3. Refill timing vs capacity (cod)

- Excess food: post-spawning weight back to the non-spawning level within 1-3 months
  (Davie, printed p.128); livers back to prespawning level ~4 months after April
  spawning (Karlsen 2006). Jobling et al. (1994, doi 10.1007/bf00128802) as cited by Davie
  (printed p.129): cod "exhibit a rapid and complete recovery of body weight when provided
  with adequate food following a period of undernutrition" [S].
- Wild: HSI rises through the whole post-spawning season to a fall maximum (Mello & Rose
  2005 [A]), i.e. ~5-6 months.
- [J] Wild refill (~5-6 months to the fall peak) looks slower than the excess-food refill
  (Davie 1-3 months is whole weight; Karlsen <= 4 months is a sampling-interval bound,
  April then August), suggesting the summer-autumn refill is food-limited (or limited by
  post-spawning feeding) rather than at capacity. The measures are not like for like.
- Ali, Nicieza & Wootton (2003) Fish Fish 4:147, doi 10.1046/j.1467-2979.2003.00120.x [A]:
  compensatory growth is partly hyperphagia; "The severity of the growth depression
  increases the duration of the hyperphagic phase rather than maximum daily feeding
  rate"; CG "is a response to restore lipid levels". Black & Love (1986, doi
  10.1007/BF00691032) and Belanger et al. (2002, doi 10.1023/A:1025461108348): no abstract
  reachable.

## 4. A regulated / optimal reserve below the maximum?

- Cod model: Jorgensen & Fiksen (2006) CJFAS 63:186, doi 10.1139/f05-209 [F]
  (data/jorgensen2006/, IIASA IR-05-067). Lines 408-412: "As immature, this individual
  prioritised growth ... Allocation to stores was low, and only minor energy reserves
  were kept to balance the risk of starvation ... allocation shifted from growth to
  stores at age 7, approximately one year prior to the first spawning migration." This is
  an optimal growth-vs-storage allocation under LIMITED (variable) food with stores
  capped at Kmax (LCImax 9 %, lines 306-310, from NEA monthly maxima 7-8 % in early winter
  "if food is abundant", Yaragina & Marshall 2000 [S]); it is not evidence of a regulated
  ceiling under excess food.
- Post & Parkinson (2001) Ecology 82:1040 [A], PROXY rainbow trout: the optimal choice
  between growth-max and storage-max depends on size (+5 % survival for growth in small
  cohorts, +7 % for storage in fast cohorts).
- Ali et al. (2003) [A]: refed fish restore lipid levels (a target), and "why animals grow
  at rates below their physiological capacity" is open.
- Salmonid lipostat [S] (already covered) is the only direct demonstration of a
  regulated set point with food in excess.
- Excess-food cod livers plateau: 8-10 % (Davie) and 10-12 % (Karlsen) under satiation
  rather than rising without bound; farmed HSI > 12 % and 9-17 % at 1.0-1.4 Cmax are
  already covered [S]; haddock HSI rises with dietary lipid (Nanton [S]), so the plateau
  is diet-dependent. Whether the plateau is regulated or anatomical is not tested in any
  source found.
- Wild comparison [D]: NEA early-winter LCI maxima 7-8 % vs the immature excess-food
  plateau 8-10 % (Davie) to 10-12 % (Karlsen) gives a wild autumn peak of ~0.6-1.0 of the
  excess-food level (crude: diet lipid and the non-linear HSI-energy relation).

## Verdict

- Cod (the only gadoid with excess-food data): NO spring low in immature fish under
  satiation. Livers sit at HSI ~7.5-12 % all year; the only seasonal movement in
  non-maturing fish is a summer-autumn decline (Davie ~1 point; Karlsen up to a third by
  August), the opposite phase to the wild spring low. The spring drop under excess food
  appears only in maturing fish, at spawning, and is refilled within 1-4 months. [F, D]
- Caveat: most immature cod data are under continuous light; Davie's first-year groups
  under natural light (satiation) also show no spring low, so a light-entrained spring
  low in immature cod is not supported, but the natural-light evidence is one cohort,
  n = 4-11 per point, 3-monthly. [F, D]
- Under excess food the cod condition peak sits in Feb-Apr (before spawning, partly gonad
  mass), opposite to the wild Sep-Dec peak; so the wild autumn peak plausibly reflects
  the wild food calendar. [J]
- Herring is the exception: the covered Berg 2024 work shows a light-entrained condition
  cycle under excess food at constant temperature (min in winter). The PROXY Pacific
  herring (lipid kept under satiation) is weaker evidence and does not override it. [S]
- Wild spring lows in the gadoids = winter acquisition deficit (cod: winter intake ~nil,
  ~80-96 % of the loss is metabolism) plus the spawning drain in matures; the slower wild
  refill suggests a food-limited autumn fill. [A, J]
- No source found shows a CHOSEN below-maximum reserve with food in excess in a target
  species. The candidates are a model of growth-vs-storage under limited food (J&F 2006),
  the salmonid lipostat [S], and the authors' interpretation in Sewall 2021 (data: lipid
  kept). [J]

## Gaps

- No constant-temperature excess-food study in any gadoid; the cod studies are all
  ambient. Only herring (Berg 2024, covered) has constant temperature.
- Pedersen & Hislop 2001 full text (immature vs mature ED by month for six target
  species) not reachable: the top gap.
- No captive excess-food series for haddock, whiting, saithe, Norway pout, hake, sprat
  or mackerel; Atlantic herring captive lipid series only via the covered photoperiod work.
- Lambert & Dutil 1997, Black & Love 1986, Jobling et al. 1994, Belanger et al. 2002 and
  Hislop 1984: no abstract or full text reached.
- Farmed HSI depends on diet lipid: the excess-food plateau is not a clean "maximum
  reserve".
