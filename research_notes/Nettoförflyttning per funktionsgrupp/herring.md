# Horizontal net displacement of herring (Clupea harengus) and sprat (Sprattus sprattus) at hour-to-day time scales

Scope note: almost all quantitative school-tracking data found are for **Norwegian spring-spawning herring (NSSH), Norwegian Sea / coast of Northern Norway**. For the **North Sea (NSAS)** a sonar tracking study exists but its speed values were not accessible. For **Skagerrak/Kattegat/Öresund (WBSS)** only seasonal timing was found, no speeds. Sprat is essentially a gap. Every number below is labelled with stock and region. Lines marked "(own calculation)" are unit conversions or derived values, not literature values.

Unit conversions used throughout (own calculation): 0.10 m/s = 0.36 km/h = 2.2 km/6 h = 9 km/d; 0.15 m/s = 3.2 km/6 h = 13 km/d; 0.45 m/s = 9.7 km/6 h = 39 km/d; 0.50 m/s = 10.8 km/6 h = 43 km/d; 0.65 m/s = 14.0 km/6 h = 56 km/d; 0.82 m/s = 17.7 km/6 h = 71 km/d; 0.94 m/s = 20.3 km/6 h = 81 km/d. The current model cap (1 cell per 6 h) is 0.046 m/s = 1.0 km/6 h = 4 km/d.

## Q1. Cruising and migration speeds of herring (BL/s, m/s)

### Takeaway
Tracked herring schools swim at a gross speed of roughly 0.7-0.9 m/s (about 2.5-3 body lengths/s). Per school over a sonar registration of minutes, net displacement is about 0.66 m/s for schools classified as migrating and about 0.5 m/s averaged over all schools (NSSH, July). Population-mean progress in one direction can be as low as 0.10-0.15 m/s. That figure comes from Misund et al. 1997, and it mixes tortuous tracks with schools heading in opposite directions, so it is a floor on per-school net speed, not a per-school feeding rate. Overwintering herring move at only 0.1-0.3 m/s.

### Cited Findings
- **NSSH, Norwegian Sea (66-68 N, 2 W-2 E), April 1995 and 1996, spring migration to the feeding areas (herring 21-39.5 cm):** schools were tracked with high-resolution sonar for about 5-65 min (1995) and 4.2-128 min (1996). Horizontal (gross) speed was 0.51-1.52 m/s, mean 0.82 m/s (SD 0.26, n = 28) in 1995, and 0.45-1.51 m/s, mean 0.94 m/s (SD 0.28, n = 25) in 1996. The authors equate this to "about 3 body lengths s-1", within the sustained swimming limit of herring. — [Misund, Melle & Fernö 1997, Sarsia 82:107-112](https://vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/31848.pdf)
- Same study: **net "average migration speed" in the mean migration direction was only 0.15 m/s (1995, mean direction 174 deg) and 0.10 m/s (1996, 165 deg)**. In the authors' words, "the schools were not swimming in straight tracks, but made frequent side-to-side movements and even turns". Some schools swam straight, others changed heading often, "and a few were circling around". The authors link the sideways movement to search for gradients in food, temperature and current. Stomach samples showed the herring were feeding at the migration depth (300-400 m). Note that the schools did not share one heading: in 1995, 6 of 10 schools around 2 E migrated west while 12 of 18 schools west of 1 E migrated south-east, and in 1996 most schools north of 67.7 N headed east while most south of it headed south. The 0.10-0.15 m/s is therefore the population vector mean in the mean direction. It folds opposing headings together with tortuosity. — [Misund et al. 1997](https://vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/31848.pdf)
- Same study: speed rose with temperature (1.0-6.3 C, r = 0.57) in 1995 but not in 1996. — [Misund et al. 1997](https://vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/31848.pdf)
- **NSSH, Norwegian Sea shelf/slope off Lofoten-Vesteralen (Northern Norway), July 1995 and 1996, feeding season (median length 0.24-0.32 m by sub-area):** about 700 schools were tracked by multi-beam sonar (Simrad SR240) from a vessel running a survey track at 10-12 knots, so each registration lasted minutes (at least 21 pings). In 1995, 502 schools were classified as 31% migrating ("moving in straight lines with a clearly defined direction"), 61% stationary ("frequently changed bearing and performed irregular or minor movements") and 8% avoiding. All schools were shallow (0-60 m, mean about 20 m) day and night, and the authors interpret them as feeding: "The stationary schools were probably foraging, and therefore frequently changing direction and moving little." — [Kvamme, Nøttestad, Fernö, Misund et al., ICES CM 2000/K:14 (full text read)](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/275962.pdf)
- Same study, speeds. "Swimming speed" is the movement of the school's acoustic centre of gravity over ground. "Migration speed" is the per-school resultant of the mean north and east components over the registration, i.e. per-school net displacement rate. The overall mean swimming speed was 0.73 m/s (SD 0.40; 2.85 BL/s), and the overall mean migration speed 0.51 m/s (SD 0.38; 2.00 BL/s). Migrating schools had a mean swimming speed of 0.83 m/s (3.24 BL/s) and a mean migration speed of 0.66 m/s (2.57 BL/s). Stationary schools had the lowest swimming speed and a lower migration speed than the other categories; exact values are only in a figure. Schools above 2.0 m/s were excluded as artefacts. — [Kvamme et al. 2000](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/275962.pdf)
- The published version gives a mean migration speed of 0.65 m/s for migrating young herring (2-6 yr) moving westward off Northern Norway. A search snippet of its full text also states "combined mean migration speed for migrating and non-migrating schools was 0.50 m s-1, or about 43 km d-1", consistent with the 0.51 m/s above. — [Kvamme et al. 2003, MEPS 247:197-210](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v247/p197-210); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250217229_Migration_patterns_in_Norwegian_spring-spawning_herring_Why_young_fish_swim_away_from_the_wintering_area_in_late_summer)
- **NSSH, cold-front area of the Norwegian Sea, under fin whale predation:** 44 schools were observed for an average of 34.8 min. They were large (mean 987 m2), dense and deep (148 m), with a "moderate swimming speed (1.1 body lengths per second)", which the authors interpret as risk-averse anti-predator behaviour. (Own calculation: 1.1 BL/s at about 0.30-0.33 m body length is about 0.33-0.36 m/s.) — [Nøttestad, Fernö, Mackinson, Pitcher & Misund 2002, ICES JMS 59:393-400](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/59/2/393/619662)
- **NSSH, Norwegian overwintering/spawning-migration area (exact site not checked), moored 307 kHz ADCP.** Overwintering, Dec 1997: horizontal speeds were mostly <0.10 m/s (largely drifting with the current until about 10:00), rising to about 0.20-0.30 m/s after dawn, with "no well-defined velocity" at night. Start of spawning migration, Jan 1998: 0.10-0.45 m/s, direction converging on about 200 deg (S-SW), with higher speeds in daylight. Overall range 0-0.50 m/s. — [Zedel, Knutsen & Patro 2003, ICES JMS 60:846-859](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/60/4/846/693863)
- **NSAS herring, North Sea, July 1991 and 1992 (HERAS-type acoustic surveys), multi-beam sonar:** 60-80% of herring schools were classed as migrating and about 20% as avoiding the vessel. Most migrating schools headed south. Mean speed "was in accordance with theoretical considerations and the results of earlier sonar studies" in the North Sea, but no number was available in the accessible abstract. — [Hafsteinsson & Misund 1995, ICES JMS 52:915-924](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/52/6/915/649261)
- Escape/avoidance context (not cruising): herring schools in purse-seine capture situations swam at 2.5-2.8 BL/s on average. — [Misund 1993, Fish Res (search snippet)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0165783693900518)

### Inferences
- Gross speed (about 0.7-0.9 m/s) and net displacement are different quantities, and the model needs net. Over a sonar registration (minutes), per-school net/gross is about 0.66/0.83 = 0.8 for migrating schools and 0.51/0.73 = 0.7 overall (own calculation from Kvamme et al. 2000).
- (own calculation, rough) If avoiding schools moved net at about 0.8 m/s, the overall mean of 0.51 m/s with a 31/61/8% mix implies stationary (foraging) schools at roughly 0.4 m/s net over minutes: (0.51 - 0.31 x 0.66 - 0.08 x 0.8) / 0.61 = 0.40. Over minutes, sonar position noise and short registrations inflate this; over hours, the frequent heading changes of foraging schools should reduce it further.
- The Misund et al. 1997 values (0.10-0.15 m/s net against 0.82-0.94 m/s gross) are a population vector mean across schools with partly opposing headings. They are not a per-school straightness index. They give a floor for how fast the herring biomass centroid moves during a mixed feeding migration, not the displacement of an individual school.
- So per-school net displacement over 1-6 h during feeding-season behaviour is **not measured** in anything found. It is bracketed by about 0.1 m/s (population-mean floor) and about 0.4-0.7 m/s (minute-scale per-school values).
- (own assumption, not sourced) Herring in the Skagerrak model area, juvenile NSAS and adult WBSS, are probably smaller (roughly 15-28 cm) than the adult NSSH above. At 1-3 BL/s that gives about 0.2-0.8 m/s gross for a 25 cm herring (own calculation), the same order as above.

### Gaps
- No Skagerrak or Kattegat speed numbers were found. For the North Sea, Hafsteinsson & Misund 1995 (NSAS, July) has values behind a paywall, and the open repository copies that were found are other papers.
- No continuous school tracks of 6-24 h exist in the sources found. Every per-school speed comes from registrations of minutes to about 2 h.

## Q2. School tracking: feeding vs travelling, diel patterns (dispersal at night)

### Takeaway
Herring schools are very dynamic over minutes. They change status every few minutes and split or join about every half hour, so a "school" is not a persistent unit over 6-24 h. Herring are in tight, deep schools by day and rise and often disperse at night. Measured swimming speed was higher at night in the spring migration (1.00 vs 0.78 m/s), but in overwintering herring there was no coherent movement at night.

### Cited Findings
- NSSH, Norwegian Sea, April 1995/96: daytime in large schools at 300-400 m depth; at night they rose to the surface and either dispersed or stayed in dense schools/shoals. Gross swimming speed was significantly higher at night (mean 1.00 m/s, SD 0.21, n = 27) than by day (0.78 m/s, SD 0.28, n = 28), with no day-night difference in direction. "The migration was faster at night than during the day", which the authors attribute to warmer surface water. — [Misund et al. 1997](https://vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/31848.pdf)
- NSSH, Norwegian Sea, 2-5 May (14 focal schools, 789 observation minutes, sidescan sonar): "some kind of change in school status occurs every 5.5 min". Predator-linked events occurred about every 27 min, and splits/joins about every 34.6 min. — [Pitcher, Misund, Fernö, Totland & Melle 1996, ICES JMS 53:449-452](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article-pdf/53/2/449/2374610/53-2-449.pdf) (via search snippets; full text not opened)
- NSSH overwintering (ADCP): fish largely drifted with the current at night and in the early morning, with active swimming of about 0.2-0.3 m/s after dawn and no well-defined velocity at night. — [Zedel et al. 2003](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/60/4/846/693863)
- Baltic Sea, March, sprat and herring: schools rise at dusk to follow isolumes, then break up once light falls below a threshold. Dissolution fits a diffusion-like model of independent random walkers, with a swimming speed of about 1 BL/s assumed (an assumption, not a measurement). — [Nilsson, Thygesen, Lundgren, Nielsen, Nielsen & Beyer 2003, Aquat Living Resour 16:317-324](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225027923_Vertical_migration_and_dispersion_of_sprat_Sprattus_prattus_and_herring_Clupea_harengus_schools_at_dusk_in_the_Baltic_Sea); [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0990744003000391)
- North Sea herring occupy surface waters at night and disperse there, mingling with other species, so they are hard to detect as echo traces. — [ICES CM 2001/Q:08](https://www.ices.dk/sites/pub/CM%20Doccuments/2001/Q/Q0801.pdf) (via search snippet)
- NSSH at the Arctic front: larger schools swam faster and in warmer water than smaller schools. — [Nøttestad et al. 2007, Marine Ecology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1439-0485.2007.00182.x) (abstract via search)

### Inferences
- Night-time dispersal works like diffusion rather than advection. At night, biomass spreads to neighbouring cells more than it relocates as a block, which fits the model's 1-cell local step. Daytime schooling in deep, dense schools is when coherent directed movement (and the "rest/hide" state) is most plausible.
- In this model, "rest/hide" maps naturally onto deep daytime schooling. Note, however, that Misund et al. 1997 found the herring kept swimming at about 0.8 m/s gross while schooling deep by day, so hiding does not equal zero movement. It mostly means low net displacement.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tracks one herring school continuously over 6-24 h. Sonar tracks last minutes to about 2 h, so displacement over 6-24 h has to be extrapolated.
- No measured night-time horizontal dispersal rate (diffusivity) for herring or sprat in the Skagerrak or Kattegat was found.

## Q3. Net displacement per day: feeding season vs migration; tortuosity; WBSS seasonal movements

### Takeaway
School-level directed migration would cover about 45-55 km/d net if sustained (NSSH, 0.51-0.66 m/s measured over minutes; own extrapolation). The population-mean progress during a mixed spring feeding migration was about 9-13 km/d (0.10-0.15 m/s, Misund et al. 1997), and that is a floor rather than a per-school rate. Overwintering herring cover a few km/d at most. For WBSS in the Skagerrak and Kattegat the literature gives seasonal timing but no speeds. The Skagerrak-to-Øresund route (about 270 km) is covered over weeks in late summer.

### Cited Findings
- NSSH, July, feeding season off Northern Norway: per-school net (migration) speed 0.66 m/s for migrating schools and 0.51 m/s overall (all categories, minute-scale registrations). The published version converts the overall combined mean of 0.50 m/s to "about 43 km d-1". 61% of schools were classified as stationary (foraging) and 31% as migrating. — [Kvamme et al. 2000](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/275962.pdf); [Kvamme et al. 2003](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250217229_Migration_patterns_in_Norwegian_spring-spawning_herring_Why_young_fish_swim_away_from_the_wintering_area_in_late_summer); [MEPS abstract](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v247/p197-210)
- NSSH spring feeding migration: population-mean net 0.10-0.15 m/s in the mean direction against 0.82-0.94 m/s gross. The authors attribute this to side-to-side movements and turns, but the schools' headings also differed between sub-areas (see Q1). — [Misund et al. 1997](https://vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/31848.pdf)
- Norwegian Sea herring feeding season: herring are spread over large areas, migrate eastward in late July-August and enter the northern Norwegian wintering areas in September. — [Kvamme et al. 2003 (search snippet)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250217229_Migration_patterns_in_Norwegian_spring-spawning_herring_Why_young_fish_swim_away_from_the_wintering_area_in_late_summer)
- **WBSS, Skagerrak, Kattegat and Öresund (the Sound, ICES SD 23):**
  - The main feeding grounds are in the Skagerrak, Kattegat and North Sea, where herring aged 2 and older live in summer.
  - "In late summer (July/August), they migrate southwards through Kattegat."
  - The Sound holds high concentrations from (late) August to March, with up to 130,000 t measured in September 1993.
  - Spawning is at Rügen in April-May, and herring migrate back to the Kattegat-Skagerrak "(partly?) through the Sound in late spring".
  - Emigration from the Sound was "abrupt over a relatively short period in Feb./Mar." in 1993/94 but more continuous in 1994/95.
  - The spring return migration north (March-May) "is not well documented".
  - Source: [Nielsen 1996, DFU report 11-96, Acoustic monitoring of herring in the Sound](https://www.aqua.dtu.dk/-/media/institutter/aqua/publikationer/forskningsrapporter_1_50/11_96_acoustic_monitoring_of_herring.pdf)
- WBSS: mature fish peak in midsummer in the eastern North Sea and outer Skagerrak, and older fish go further into the North Sea. In Q3, groups of 2+ fish gather in the southern Kattegat before overwintering in the Øresund and Køge Bay. — [Gröhsler et al. / ICES JMS fsp185 (search summary)](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/icesjms/fsp185)
- The Sound was occupied from August to March with a peak in autumn, and larger fish left first. — [Nielsen et al. 2001, cited in Polte et al./ICES JMS fst166 (search summary)](https://doi.org/10.1093/icesjms/fst166)
- WBSS mixes with NSAS in the Skagerrak and Kattegat in summer. — [Clausen et al. 2015, J Fish Biol](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jfb.12571); [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.589242/pdf)
- NSAS (North Sea): in July most schools are migrating south. — [Hafsteinsson & Misund 1995](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/52/6/915/649261)

### Inferences
- (own calculation) Great-circle distance from the model area centre (58.05 N, 10.6 E) to the central Sound (Ven, about 55.9 N, 12.7 E) is about 270 km. If the WBSS southward migration in July-August took 2-8 weeks, the mean net rate of the stock's centre of mass would be about 5-20 km/d. That is far below the school-level directed speed (40-55 km/d), which shows that population-level relocation is slow even while individual schools travel fast. The stock-level rate is an order-of-magnitude bound, not a measured value.
- (own calculation) For the 60 x 60 km model area, a herring school in directed migration at 0.5-0.65 m/s could cross the whole domain in about 1-1.5 days (60 km / 43-56 km/d). At the population-mean rate of 0.10-0.15 m/s (Misund floor), the herring biomass centroid would need about 5-7 days.

### Gaps
- No tagging (DST or acoustic) data on daily displacement were found for NSAS, WBSS or Skagerrak herring. Herring are hard to tag, and the literature relies on acoustic surveys.
- No feeding-season net displacement per day was found for North Sea or Skagerrak herring.
- The duration of the WBSS Kattegat passage (days or weeks) was not found.

## Q4. Sprat (secondary)

### Takeaway
I found no measured horizontal school speeds or daily displacement for sprat. Only assumed values (1 BL/s) and qualitative behaviour are available.

### Cited Findings
- Baltic sprat and herring schools disperse at dusk like random walkers, with 1 BL/s assumed to estimate diffusivity. The authors note that sonar tracking could provide local swimming speeds. — [Nilsson et al. 2003](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225027923_Vertical_migration_and_dispersion_of_sprat_Sprattus_prattus_and_herring_Clupea_harengus_schools_at_dusk_in_the_Baltic_Sea)
- Laboratory sustained swimming performance has been measured for juvenile sprat and herring at 18-33 psu and 16-19 C, but I could not retrieve the values. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230225075_Swimming_performance_of_juvenile_sprat_Sprattus_sprattus_L_and_herring_Clupea_harengus_L_at_different_salinities)
- Overwintering sprat in a Norwegian fjord show normal and asynchronous diel vertical migration. — [Ecology of overwintering sprat, Prog Oceanogr](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0079661115001755)

### Inferences
- (own calculation) A 10-14 cm sprat at about 1-3 BL/s swims at 0.10-0.40 m/s gross, which is lower than herring. If the model lumps sprat into `pelagic_fish`, the herring numbers are an upper bound.

### Gaps
- No measured sprat school speed, migration speed or net displacement was found.

## Q5. Synthesis for the model: transit reach k and local scale (all own calculation unless cited)

### Takeaway
The current cap of 1 km per 6 h (0.046 m/s) is below every measured herring movement category except passive overwintering drift. It is 2-3x below even the population-mean floor of 0.10-0.15 m/s (2-3 km per 6 h) and about 11-14x below per-school directed migration (0.51-0.66 m/s gives 11-14 km per 6 h). The suggested settings are a transit reach of **k of about 10-11 cells per 6 h (range 5-14; 10 if rounded down, 11 if rounded to nearest at 0.5 m/s)** and a local step of 1-3 cells per 6 h, a model choice since per-school feeding displacement over hours is not measured. For a 1-h step, use **k of about 2 cells (range 1-2)** and a local step of 1 cell.

### Cited Findings (anchors)
- Per-school net migration speed (minute-scale registrations), NSSH July: 0.66 m/s for migrating schools and 0.51 m/s for all schools; 0.83 and 0.73 m/s gross. — [Kvamme et al. 2000](https://www.vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/275962.pdf); [Kvamme et al. 2003](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v247/p197-210)
- Population-mean net progress during a mixed spring feeding migration: 0.10-0.15 m/s against 0.8-0.9 m/s gross, NSSH. This is a floor, not a per-school rate. — [Misund et al. 1997](https://vliz.be/imisdocs/publications/ocrd/31848.pdf)
- Start of spawning migration out of the overwintering area: 0.10-0.45 m/s directed; overwintering below about 0.1-0.3 m/s. — [Zedel et al. 2003](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/60/4/846/693863)

### Inferences
- **Transit reach k, 6-h step:** the net displacement of a step spent travelling, at 0.45-0.66 m/s, is 9.7-14.3 km. The central value 0.5 m/s gives 10.8 km, so **k is about 10-11 (plausible range 5-14)**. This extrapolates minute-scale per-school net speeds to 6 h of continuous travel. Kvamme's migrating schools held a heading within 45 deg over the registration, and holding it for 6 h is not documented. The low end (5) corresponds to about 0.23 m/s: small Skagerrak herring at about 1 BL/s, or the slower ADCP migration speeds. Do not set k from gross speed (0.8-0.9 m/s, 17-20 km per 6 h). Even for migrating schools net/gross is about 0.8 over minutes (Kvamme), and over hours it is lower.
- **Local step, 6-h step:** per-school feeding displacement over hours is unmeasured. It is bracketed by the population floor of 0.10-0.15 m/s (2-3 km per 6 h) and minute-scale stationary-school values of roughly 0.4 m/s (about 9 km per 6 h, own rough derivation in Q1), and tortuosity over hours pushes toward the low end. A local step of 1-3 cells per 6 h is therefore a model choice. 1 cell is at or slightly below the floor; 2 cells (about 0.09 m/s) matches the Misund population floor. Daytime deep schooling, night-time diffusive dispersal and frequent turning all keep net displacement small. Overwintering-like low activity (<0.1 m/s, about 2 km per 6 h, partly current drift) fits within 1 cell.
- **1-h step:** directed 0.45-0.65 m/s gives 1.6-2.3 km/h, so **k is about 2 (range 1-2)**. Feeding net 0.10-0.15 m/s gives 0.36-0.54 km/h, which is below 1 cell, so a 1-cell local step per hour (0.28 m/s if taken every tick) would let a continuously moving feeding school travel about 2-3x too far per day. That is acceptable only if herring rest/hide a substantial share of ticks, as they do in the model. With the local step taken in about half of the ticks, straight-line moves would give about 12 km/d, comparable to the Misund population floor of 9-13 km/d (own estimate, assuming 50% active ticks).
- **Scaling rule:** k should scale linearly with step length (k = v_net x dt / 1 km, rounded, minimum 1): about 1.8 k per hour at 0.5 m/s. For example, 1 h gives 2, 3 h gives 5, and 6 h gives 11 (rounded to nearest; 10 if rounded down). The local step should not scale linearly, because tortuous feeding movement scales sub-linearly (diffusive, roughly with the square root of dt). The local step can stay at 1 cell for all step lengths from 1 to 6 h.
- **Daily caps as a sanity check:** directed migration allows up to about 40-55 km/d (about the whole 60-km domain in 1-1.5 d). The population-mean floor during a mixed feeding migration is about 9-13 km/d, and stock-level seasonal relocation of WBSS about 5-20 km/d (a rough bound). A model where herring transit 4 x 10 km in one day continuously would match only fully directed migration days.
- Model caveat: one transit jump of k cells in one of four directions cannot move diagonally within a step (a diagonal net displacement of k/sqrt2 per axis needs two steps), and it ignores the gradual heading changes seen in Misund et al. 1997. That is acceptable at a 1 km resolution, since the literature numbers are themselves +/- 50%.

### Gaps
- None of the anchor speeds come from the Skagerrak or Kattegat. The recommendation transfers NSSH data (larger fish, open ocean) to smaller WBSS/NSAS herring in a coastal shelf sea, which probably overestimates speed there. That argues for the lower half of the k range (about 6-10 per 6 h).
- No measured data were found on the fraction of time herring schools spend in directed transit versus local feeding over a day in summer. Proxies from minute-scale tracks: Hafsteinsson & Misund (1995) classed 60-80% of North Sea (NSAS) schools in July as "migrating", and Kvamme et al. (2000) classed 31% of NSSH schools off Northern Norway in July as migrating and 61% as stationary/foraging. These are snapshot proportions of schools, which could be read as a rough share of time spent in transit (about 0.3-0.7), not measured time budgets.
