THE DECOMPOSITION USED BELOW (J). Between "on the surface" (the time shares of part C) and "eaten" sit:
  E = share of the time-on-surface during which the animal is actually detectable/approachable
      (not buried, not tucked in a burrow mouth or shell, not in a crevice);
  p_rel = capture success on such an exposed animal relative to a non-burying, non-armoured
      reference prey of the same size (the model's p 0.3-0.4 is the reference);
  S = share of the biomass inside the size window the fish can swallow.
The model's p already contains the reference capture failure; a lab attack rate (Persson 2012) is the
PRODUCT (encounter x E x p) and must not be multiplied on top of p again. Ratios between two prey
measured in the same arena with the same fish are the usable quantity.

1.2 Ljungberg, Nilsson & Persson 2012. Prey selectivity by juvenile Atlantic cod in three coastal habitat
    types. MEPS 466:215-223. doi:10.3354/meps09932   [F]
    File: ../../Attackrater ur mötesteori/data/benth_validation/ljungberg2012_meps466.pdf (not copied).
  Same tanks and fish (177 +- 9 mm TL), 7.4 +- 0.8 C, 15 mm sand layer; light "0.73 uE m-2 s-1 at the
  water surface" vs darkness; 20 Crangon (38.5 +- 5.0 mm) + 20 Palaemon (36.2 +- 4.9 mm) per 2-h trial
  (54 ind m-2 of each); recovery check 100 % / 99.4 %.
  - "the effects of light regime and the interaction between habitat and light were not significant
    (F1,35 = 0.643, p = 0.428 and F2,35 = 0.793, p = 0.460, respectively), indicating that functional
    response parameters, estimated in light conditions, were valid also in dark conditions."
  - Manly-Chesson alpha for Palaemon (Fig. 2 caption): predicted from a ratios 0.93 sand, 0.75 eelgrass,
    0.60 bladderwrack; observed "bladderwrack light 0.45 and dark 0.33, artificial eelgrass light 0.63
    and dark 0.54 and sand light 0.76 and dark 0.83."
  D: Manly's alpha with depletion is ln(survival) based, so alpha_P/alpha_C = ratio of per-capita
    mortality rates = effective a_P/a_C in the two-prey trial. Effective a(Crangon)/a(Palaemon):
      sand light 0.24/0.76 = 0.32; sand dark 0.17/0.83 = 0.20;
      eelgrass light 0.59; eelgrass dark 0.85; bladderwrack light 1.22; bladderwrack dark 2.0.
    On sand the burier is taken at 0.2-0.3 of the non-burier's per-capita rate when both are present
    (vs 0.086 from the single-prey fits). Range on sand for the burier/exposed ratio (1.1 + 1.2):
    0.09-0.32.
  - Darkness did not cut cod's catch of either shrimp in a 0.38 m2 tank (J: in a tank this small,
    encounter is not search-limited; the test says capture-given-encounter does not need light, i.e.
    lateral line/smell/touch suffice at < 0.5 m; it does not test detection distance). The authors
    attribute the extra Crangon at night to its "nocturnal activity ... in a non-preferred habitat".
  - h per prey from Table 1 x energy: Crangon 1824 J, Palaemon 1898 J per individual.

1.3 Staniland 1995. PhD thesis, Univ. Leicester (whiting feeding behaviour; figshare 10142354)   [F]
    File: ../../Attackrater ur mötesteori/data/gap_vision/staniland_whiting_thesis.pdf (not copied;
    already summarised in addendum_vision_light.md "Gap 2"; only what is new is added here).
  - Prey was Crangon crangon presented on a SOLID white plastic floor: "In the wild the shrimp would
    probably tail flip away and then bury itself into the sandy substrate over which it normally lives.
    As the tank that the experiments were held had a solid plastic bottom this was not possible." and
    "The shrimp prey would also normally be able to hide themselves by burying in the substrate which
    was not possible in these experiments."
  - So the strike success (small whiting, empty stomach: 0.8 on 5.9-6.5 cm shrimp, 0.4 on 7.5-8.0 cm;
    falling with stomach fullness; zero for small whiting with one large shrimp eaten; large whiting
    higher) is capture success on an EXPOSED, non-burying Crangon (a reference value for p_rel = 1).
  - "The whiting feeding on the shrimps tended to attack them on the bottom of the tank. The shrimps
    rarely moved off the bottom". "The main methods used to detect the prey were probably sight and
    vibration as the response usually occurred to a moving shrimp." Room light 0.6-1.55 uE m-2 s-1
    (controlled-temperature room) / 14-20 (hut), 15-17 C (thesis ch. 3 holding conditions).
  - J (decomposition): Persson's lumped burier/exposed ratio ~0.1 (0.05-0.35) on sand, with capture of an
    exposed Crangon at 0.4-0.8 per strike (Staniland) and turbot/brill 0.72-0.73 per attack (Holmes,
    cited in the encounter note), implies most of the burier's deficit is in DETECTION / EXPOSURE (the
    shrimp is not seen or felt while buried and motionless), not in a lower strike success once found.

1.4 Holmes 1979. PhD thesis, Univ. Stirling (flatfish feeding)   [F] (text extract held by an earlier
    agent; the encounter note cites the PDF). New items only:
  - Response to a stationary shrimp is weak: "There is no significant difference between a moving blank
    glass tube and a stationary stimulus whether it bears a horizontal or a vertical shrimp. This
    demonstrates the low level of responsiveness to a stationary shrimp."
  - Burying is not a full refuge from a nearby predator: "pursued shrimps, if not caught, immediately
    quickly bury themselves in the sand. The method of burying agitates the sand particles ... and
    turbot stalking shrimps were often observed to lunge at this burying movement in the sand, even when
    no part of the shrimp was visible, and successfully capture the prey."
  J: detection keys on MOVEMENT (supports w = 0.6 L for moving prey); a motionless buried shrimp is
  outside w; a shrimp burying in front of the predator is still caught.

1.5 Arnott & Pihl 2000. Selection of prey size and prey species by 1-group cod: effects of satiation
    level and prey handling times. MEPS 198:225-238. doi:10.3354/meps198225   [F]
    data/D_epifauna/meps198_225/
  Lab (Kristineberg, W Sweden) and field (Gullmarsvik Bay, shallow); cod 230-289 mm in the lab.
  - "cod consumed more plaice than shrimps and more shrimps than crabs" (meal size, single-species).
  - Crab handling time independent of the prey:cod length ratio: "With Carcinus maenas, a median
    handling time of 9 s"; shrimp and plaice handling times rose with the ratio.
  - Crab defence is at the mouth, not in the chase: "Crabs with a P:C length ratio of between 0.06 and
    0.09 were frequently attacked but then often rejected (forcibly ejected from the mouth), even if the
    cod had an empty stomach." (P:C ratio for crabs = carapace WIDTH / cod TL.)
  - "crabs were sometimes retrieved from cod stomachs still alive and mobile" ; "no evidence was found of
    prey being crushed by the cod during handling".
  - Field: crabs eaten at CW/TL 0.04-0.10, selectivity peaks at 0.07-0.09; shrimps eaten at TL/TL 0.05-
    0.17, peaks 0.09-0.15; plaice 0.18-0.29, peak 0.24. Lab acceptance: crabs 0.03-0.09, Crangon 0.08-0.22
    (the full range offered). See section 3 for the size window.

1.6 Rindorf 2003. Diel feeding pattern of whiting in the North Sea. MEPS 249:265-276.
    doi:10.3354/meps249265   [F]   data/D_epifauna/rindorf2003/ (earlier notes had the abstract only)
  - Bottom trawl, early September 1988/89, 5 locations at 44, 46, 53, 73 and 137 m (16.7, 13.7, 10.6,
    8.2, 8.6 C), one haul every 4 h for 48-72 h; occurrence (presence/absence) GLMs, not rates.
  - Prey groups: "The shrimps found were a mixture of Pandalus and Crangon spp."; "'other benthic
    invertebrates' were mainly polychaetes, hermit crabs and brittle stars with some molluscs and Norway
    lobsters".
  - "The prey groups for which the majority of the diel variation could be explained by a common pattern
    included crabs, 'other benthic invertebrates' and to some extent dab"; "The common diel pattern for
    crabs explained 31% of the deviance".
  - Shrimp: no common pattern; at Location 2 "shrimp appeared to be eaten in the daylight hours and into
    the early evening", which "mirrors the demersal availability" of Pandalus montagui (ascends at night;
    Bergstrom 2000 cited). "Crangon crangon is more active during the night and spends part of the
    daylight hours buried in the sediment (Hagerman 1970, Ansell et al. 1999)" [S for Hagerman].
  - Deep station (137 m, 8.6 C, Location 5): "significant diel variation was only found in the prey
    group 'other crustaceans'"; "The lack of 'other benthic invertebrates' was mirrored by the virtual
    absence of both crabs and flatfish, suggesting perhaps that whiting at this location did not forage
    on the bottom or that the density of benthic prey was low. The lack of benthic foraging may have been
    linked to the greater depth of this location."
  - Conclusion: "whiting at 4 of the 5 locations were feeding on bottom dwelling prey at night, whereas
    pelagic and free swimming prey were captured mainly during daylight. This pattern could be due either
    to the ability of the bottom dwelling prey to avoid the predator during daylight, the increased
    availability of other prey during the day, or the necessity of vision to capture-success of free
    swimming prey."
  - Pedersen (2000) cited: at 90 m and 6 C "around 85% of the stomach content (by weight) of whiting
    between 20 and 35 cm was fish prey", demersal whiting fed by day.
  J: the night peak for crabs and benthic invertebrates on shallow North Sea ground is consistent with
  prey emergence at night (exposure rises) and with chemo-tactile capture working in the dark. The one
  deep, cold station (closest to the Skagerrak, 137 m) shows no benthic signal at all. No ratio can be
  read off (occurrence curves only; figures not digitised).

1.8 Ansell, Comely & Robb 1999. MEPS 176:115-130. doi:10.3354/meps176115   [F]
    data/D_epifauna/ansell1999/  (Scottish sandy beach, shallow)
  - "many macrocrustacean individuals are buried and inactive during this period [day]. Underwater
    television recordings made in the nearby Dunstaffnage Bay (A. D. Ansell unpubl. obs.) have shown
    individual C. maenas burrowing into the substratum at dawn and remaining buried within the field of
    view of the camera for periods of up to 14 h in summer. Buried C. crangon show a much reduced
    tendency to respond to mechanical stimulation by tail flipping compared to individuals on the surface
    (K. Smith unpubl. obs)."
  - Beam-trawl catches: Crangon crangon, Liocarcinus depurator and Pagurus bernhardus "showed no
    significant change in numbers with time [of day]"; Carcinus and L. holsatus more at night.
  - Cites Gibson et al. 1998 (Swedish microtidal beach): "C. crangon were mostly active during the day".
  J: in shallow water the burial rhythm of Crangon is variable (night-active in Scotland, day-active on
  a Swedish beach); burial suppresses the escape response (buried shrimp is easier to take IF found).

1.9 Meager, Solbakken, Utne-Palm, Shen & Ferno 2005. Effects of turbidity on the reactive distance,

  - Prey: the mysid Praunus neglectus. "Juvenile cod using chemical cues were able to locate mysids from

    predation rates on free-ranging mysids, but predation rates were significantly lower in dark

2. EXPOSED SHARE OF THE TIME ON THE SURFACE, PER TAXON (question 1)
=====================================================================================================
Part C's time shares (Pandalus 0.75 of the day on the bottom; crabs/hermits 0.6; Munida 0.5; small
shrimps and mysids 0.6) are built from trawl catch ratios, i.e. they ALREADY exclude buried and
burrow-bound animals (a buried crab is not caught). So "E" here is what is left: of the animals that
are out, how many are detectable by a passing fish.

2.1 Swimming crabs, Liocarcinus depurator (the brachyuran of 100-300 m mud, J from part C)
  Glass 1985, PhD thesis Univ. Glasgow, Loch Sween 3-30 m, 217 dives / 163 h, Feb 1981-Jun 1982   [F]
  File: ../../Refugium och utbyte i bentos/data/glass1985_thesis/1985Glassphd.pdf (not copied; part B
  of the Refugium note used it only for movement).
  - Dives "generally carried out at approximately mid-day"; "a number of dives were made at different
    times during one day to investigate the possibility that abundance and distribution of crabs varied
    with time of day. No such variation was observed."
  - "Of the crabs found on or in the mud/silt substrate, the majority of crabs were found standing on
    top of the substrate (Eilean Mhartan 309; n = 422; 73%: Port Lunna, 92; n = 154; 60%) as opposed to
    buried or semi-buried."
  - "At Eilean Mhartan, 422 crabs were observed in exposed areas; 90% (382) of these were raised on the
    sediment with only 10% (40) collapsed or semi-buried. At Port Lunna 89% (105) of crabs in exposed
    areas were raised above the substrate."
  - "Very few crabs were observed partially buried or sitting on the mud with most animals either
    standing upright or totally buried. It is postulated that total burial in the mud is an effective
    form of concealment for crabs which are not foraging".
  - Foraging on open mud: "most of these animals did not appear to be moving directly to cover, but
    appeared to be slowly probing the sediment with one or both chelipeds."
  - Anti-predator responses (to divers): "Instant burial response. This type of response was shown only
    by juvenile crabs (< 20 mm) ... Complete burial by this method could be achieved within approximately
    three seconds." Large crabs: "rapid zig-zag swimming movements across the top of the sediment ...
    sediment was disturbed into suspension thereby reducing visibility." "Small swimming crabs (< 30 mm)
    rarely showed this protean behaviour and tended to adopt the instant burial response".
  - Natural predators named: "cephalopods and ground feeding fish such as poor cod (Trisopterus
    minutus), dogfish, plaice and skate"; seals.
  - Goneplax depth switch (cited by Glass): Atkinson 1974 "suggested that Goneplax from different depths
    may be active away from their burrows at different times, being day active in deep water where light
    intensity is low and night active in shallow water"; "Farmer (1974) ... trawled more Goneplax during
    the day than night from deep water." [S]
  D/J: by day on mud 60-73 % of L. depurator stand fully exposed and 27-40 % are buried or semi-buried
  (direct count; shallow, lit). This independently supports part C's 0.6 (0.5-0.8). Of those out, ~0.9
  stand raised (E ~0.9; J reading: the 90 % "raised" and the 73 % "standing ... as opposed to buried"
  share the denominator 422 but do not partition it the same way, so 0.9 is not a clean count). Inactive crabs on the surface (Atkinson's Lundy dives, part C) are stationary:
  Holmes 1979 shows weak responses to stationary prey, so a stationary crab is detected less within w
  (no ratio; J E 0.7-1.0 for crabs out of the sediment).

2.2 Hermit crabs (Anapagurus laevis, Pagurus pubescens at 100-200 m; P. bernhardus)
  - No burial or activity rhythm found (part C Q4.12; one more search here: nothing).
  - Beam-trawl catches of P. bernhardus showed "no significant change in numbers with time" of day
    (Ansell et al. 1999 [F], shallow beach).
  - Shell is not a refuge from cod or haddock (diet evidence, section 3.3). J: E ~1 (they do not bury),
    time out = part C's 0.6 (judgement there); the gastropod shell is an armour/size issue (section 3).

2.3 Munida (M. rugosa, M. sarsi)
  - Part C (Zainal 1990; Atkinson): occupies vacated burrows and burrow mouths; "remain at or near the
    opening"; does not burrow itself. Rhythm site-dependent. Nothing new found.
  J: E 0.6-0.9 of time "out": an animal at a burrow mouth retreats when touched (door-keeping, as in
  Nephrops); no measurement.

2.4 Northern shrimp Pandalus borealis
  - Part C: 0.75 (0.65-0.85) of the day in the bottom layer; ovigerous females stay down; schooling.
  - No record of burial was found (Shumway 1985 synopsis in part C describes it as a benthic/
    hyperbenthic schooling shrimp; ASMFC habitat sheet: "organic-rich mud bottoms", nothing on burial;
    Bergstrom 2000 not obtained). Rindorf 2003 [F] treats Pandalus as "at or near the bottom" by day,
    ascending at night.
  J: E ~1 when in the bottom layer (no burial; moving and schooling, i.e. inside w by the lateral line).

2.5 Small shrimps (Crangon allmanni, Philocheras, Pontophilus, Processa)
  - Processa: night/day trawl factor ~100 on the 100 m Mediterranean shelf, falling with depth (part C,
    Aguzzi & Bahamon 2009): "Buriers presented a more elevated daytime error of sampling on shelf and
    slope. Their occurrence increased at night up to 100 times on the shelf, with a smaller sampling error
    on the slope (up to 4 times more elevated during day than night)." Bahamon 2009: "No clearly
    discernible diel pattern was found with the dampening of light intensity with depth".
  - Crangon crangon (shallow): buried by day in Scotland, day-active on a Swedish beach (Ansell 1999 [F],
    citing Gibson et al. 1998); emergence light-cued (Addison 2001 [F]); buried shrimp have "a much
    reduced tendency to respond to mechanical stimulation by tail flipping" (Ansell 1999, unpubl. obs.
    cited). Al-Adhub & Naylor 1975 [A]: "evidence of nocturnal and tidal rhythms of emergence ... which
    deserves further experimental investigation" (introduction; results not read).
  - Pinn & Ansell 1993 [A]: "Particle size was found to be a major influencing factor on the degree of
    burial achieved by C. crangon, but no significant effect of shrimp size" (direction for mud not
    read).
  - Crangon allmanni: no burial or diel data (one targeted search; part C also found none).
  - Lab, light, sand (Persson 2012; Ljungberg 2012, section 1): the burier's PER-CAPITA predation rate is
    0.09-0.32 of an equal-sized non-burier; in darkness 0.20 (sand, two-prey trial).
  J: two non-identical readings exist and must not both be applied:
    (i) part C's time-on-surface 0.6 x E ~1 x p_rel ~1 (if part C's 0.6 is believed for the deep
        floor), or
    (ii) the lab product (time out x E x p_rel) ~0.2 (0.09-0.35), measured in light on sand where a
        light-cued burier is buried most of the time.
  At 100-300 m the light cue is weak (1e-3 lx by day) and the Mediterranean slope buriers show night/
  day contrasts of 1-4, not 100: the deep floor sits between (i) and (ii). Central J 0.35 (0.1-0.6)
  for the product, i.e. E x p_rel ~0.6 (0.2-1) on top of part C's 0.6.

2.6 Mysids (Erythrops, Mysis mixta, Boreomysis, Lophogaster)
  - Part C (Kaartvedt 1989): Lophogaster "is known to burrow into the sediment during daylight";
    Lophogaster and Leptomysis leave the bottom for the whole night; part of Boreomysis stays. Vertically
    migrating mysids "not abundant at the shelf and trench locations" (Brattegard, cited).
  - Suprabenthos 76-97 % in 0-50 cm by day (Cunha 1997, cited in part C).
  - Persson 2012: a on Neomysis (sand) 57 cm2/s vs 21 on Palaemon and 1.8 on Crangon: the most exposed
    of the three prey (and the smallest; per-capita rate 2.7x the exposed shrimp, 32x the burier).
  - Meager 2005 [A]: fewer mysids taken in darkness; chemical detection range > visual.
  J: E ~1 for the non-burying near-bottom mysids by day (they swim in the 0-0.5 m layer inside a
  demersal fish's w); ~0 for Lophogaster by day. Off-bottom at night = out of the benthivore band.

3.1 Cod gape on crabs: Arnott & Pihl 2000 [F] (section 1.5)
  - Lab acceptance (cod 230-289 mm, Carcinus maenas, CW 7.2-26.0 mm): "Cod consumed Carcinus maenas prey
    within the P:C length ratio of 0.03 to 0.09 (up to 2.3 % cod's W). ... the upper limit represents the
    upper limit that cod would accept. Crabs with a P:C length ratio of between 0.06 and 0.09 were
    frequently attacked but then often rejected (forcibly ejected from the mouth), even if the cod had an
    empty stomach." (P:C = carapace width / cod TL.)
  - Field (Gullmarsvik Bay): range eaten 0.04-0.09, 0.04-0.10, 0.02-0.12 (June, July, September);
    selectivity peaked at 0.07, 0.09, 0.08; small crabs avoided ("avoidance (or inaccessibility) of the

3.2 Armour and handling
  - No crushing by cod; crabs sometimes alive in the stomach; rejection at the mouth at CW/TL 0.06-0.09
    (Arnott & Pihl 2000 [F]).
  - Whiting: the small-mouthed, mostly piscivorous predator; Staniland's whiting took shrimps, not crabs
    (no crab trials).
  J: crab armour acts as a size threshold at the mouth (rejection), not as a per-strike failure inside
  the window: p_rel ~1 inside the window for cod (handling 9 s, size-independent), ~0 beyond it.

  - Pedersen 1999, J Fish Biol 55:1096, doi:10.1111/j.1095-8649.1999.tb00744.x [A]: "Pelagic North Sea
    whiting ... preyed upon (pelagic) sprat and sandeel, while demersal whiting preyed upon (demersal)
    Norway pout and herring." "The diet of whiting shifted from amphipods and mysids to fish with
    increasing predator length, and the length of prey consumed increased significantly with length of
    whiting."
  - Pedersen 2000, J Fish Biol 57:402, doi:10.1111/j.1095-8649.2000.tb02180.x [A]: "Pelagic North Sea
    whiting fed at night, while demersal whiting fed by day." Rindorf 2003 [F] citing it: 90 m, 6 C,
    "around 85% of the stomach content (by weight) of whiting between 20 and 35 cm was fish prey".
  - Staniland 1995 [F] ch. 2 (northern North Sea, small whiting): 10-14 cm whiting by weight: sandeel

    are relatively large the fish may not have been able to consume more than one at a time".

    "feeding equally from the pelagic and the benthic zones"; Pawson 1974 cited: whiting "successful at
    locating buried food and digging it up".
  - Addison 2001 [F]: whiting catch rates "much higher at night" on a Crangon ground (shallow Wash).
