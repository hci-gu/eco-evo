Q1. CROPPING AS AN EXCHANGE (sublethal loss of regrowable parts)
======================================================================================

1a. Amphiura arm regrowth RATE
- Skold & Gunnarsson 1996, MEPS 142:203-214, doi 10.3354/meps142203 [F]
  data/skold_gunnarsson1996_meps142/. Kattegat/Skagerrak box cores, lab 2.5 mo (Apr-Jun 1993).
  Table 3: mean regenerated arm length of A. filiformis 9.2-13.1 mm over 77 d (n 9-16 per cell,
  four site x food treatments). "Regeneration rates are calculated by dividing regenerated arm
  lengths by 77 d for A. filiformis and 66 d for Amphiura chiajei."
  -> 0.12-0.17 mm per arm per day (J arithmetic: 9.2/77 = 0.12, 13.1/77 = 0.17).
  "Amphiura chiajei regenerated only ca 20% of the increase observed for Amphiura filiformis".
  Mass rates: "The regeneration rates of Amphiura filiformis, ranging from 2.9 to 3.8 ug dry wt
  arm-1 d-1 in this experiment (Table 3) are within the range reported by Salzwedel (7.1; 1974),
  Andreasson (4.8; 1990), and Nilsson & Skold (3.7; 1996), but lower than 82 ug dry wt arm-1 d-1
  reported by O'Connor et al. (1986)." [F; the cited values are S]
- Biressi et al. 2010, Zoomorphology (two ophiuroids) [S, search snippet]: "0.99 mm/week for
  A. filiformis" = 0.14 mm/d (J).
- Univ. Padova record (conference abstract, c. 2000) [S, snippet]: "as much as 1-2mm of re-growth
  recorded in Amphiura filiformis over 10 days at 10-11 C" = 0.1-0.2 mm/d (J).
- Czarkwiani et al. 2016, Front Zool 13:18, doi 10.1186/s12983-016-0149-x [F via WebFetch extract
  of PMC4841056, first 100k characters]: 14 C, arm cut 1 cm from disc. Wound healing 1-4 d post amputation; regenerative bud
  5.3 +- 0.9 d; "50% differentiation index (2-3 weeks post-amputation)", "95% ... (4-5 weeks
  post-amputation)". No mm/d given. (The 50/95 % index is the share of the regenerate that is
  differentiated, not regrown length.)
- Dupont & Thorndyke 2006, J Exp Biol 209:3873-3881, doi 10.1242/jeb.02445 [F via WebFetch
  extract of the journal HTML, first 100k characters; PDF not saved]: 14 C, whole animals cut at
  length lost (LL) 5-60 mm: regeneration rate rose "from about 0.6 to 3.3 mm/week" as LL rose
  from 5 to 60 mm (extract's wording), "RR increased exponentially with LL (P<0.01)"; arms cut
  near the disc regrow faster in length but recover function more slowly. Literature range cited
  there: 0.08-0.45 mm/d.
  J: fitting the two end points (RR = 0.6 exp(0.031 (LL-5)) mm/week) gives ~0.70 mm/week at LL 10
  mm and ~0.96 mm/week at LL 20 mm, i.e. 0.10-0.14 mm/d for tip losses, and LL/RR = ~100-145 d
  to regrow a lost 10-20 mm tip at 14 C.
- Nilsson & Skold 1996 (JEMBE 199:193, hypoxia) and Bowmer & Keegan 1983 (Mar Biol 74:65-71,
  Galway Bay) were NOT obtained; only their values as cited above.
=> Regrowth time scale (J): an exposed tip of 10-20 mm (Loo et al. 1996; MarLIN 30-40 mm) at
   0.10-0.17 mm/d is regrown in ~60-200 d; central ~100-120 d (Dupont & Thorndyke tip-loss rates
   give 100-145 d at 14 C; Skold & Gunnarsson 0.12-0.17 mm/d give 60-170 d).
   A. chiajei (deposit feeder, 100 m) ~5x slower (Skold & Gunnarsson: 20 % of the increase).
   At Mareld bottom temperatures (~6 C at 280 m, Dauwe 1998) rates are probably at or below the
   lab values (10-14 C) - J, no temperature series found.

1b. How much Amphiura mass is in regrowth / cropped (already known, cited for completeness)
- Skold, Loo & Rosenberg 1994 MEPS 103:81 [F in earlier note]: regenerated tissue 12-30 % (mean
  22 %) of adult biomass; 13.3 % of total production to arm regeneration; P/B 0.46 /yr.
  -> regeneration flux ~0.13 x 0.46 = 0.06 x B per yr (J, earlier note).
- Duineveld & Van Noort 1986 [A]: dab eat 0.84 g WW/m2/yr = 420 arms = 6 % of the arm
  population per yr; only 4 % of individuals intact; 20 % of arms with recent distal regeneration.
- O'Connor et al. 1986 Ophelia 26 [A/S]: 16 % of energy to arm regeneration; "annual somatic
  growth is almost entirely restricted to arm regeneration" (Galway Bay, low recruitment).
- Skold & Gunnarsson 1996 [F] reconstructs Skold et al. 1994: total production 9.46 g dry wt
  m-2 yr-1; "Thirteen percent of that was due to arm regeneration and 69 % due to disk growth".

1c. Bivalve siphons
- Zwarts, Blomert, Spaak & de Vries 1994, JEMBE 183:193-212 ("Feeding radius, burying depth
  and siphon size of Macoma balthica and Scrobicularia plana") [A, marineinfo 21184]:
  "A comparison between species reveals that the weight of the extended inhalant siphon per cm
  amounts, on average, to 0.6% of the total body weight." "Siphon cropping therefore results in
  an immediate reduction in siphon length, unless the siphon is heavy and a reserve is available."
  -> a 1-2 cm cropped siphon tip = 0.6-1.2 % of body mass (J).
- Zwarts & Wanink 1989 Mar Biol 100:227-240 (siphon size and burying depth) [A via search
  summary only]: siphon weight relative to body weight falls in larger size classes; siphon size
  "one of the main factors determining burying depth".
- Field experiment on Scrobicularia (marineinfo 21346, Zwarts and co-workers) [A]: "Heavy
  cropping of the siphon reduced the body weight"; "Siphon-cropping reduces the burying depth and
  makes the bivalves more accessible for wading birds".
- de Goeij, Luttikhuizen, van der Meer & Piersma 2001, Oecologia 126:500-506 [S via search
  summary; abstract elided by publisher]: juvenile-plaice siphon nipping - clams lost siphon mass
  and came closer to the surface; "the speed of siphon recovery after nipping varied greatly
  between experiments". No rate obtained.
- de Vlas 1985, NJSR 19:147-164 (siphon regeneration production, Macoma) - NOT obtained
  (VLIZ: non-open access); no number. Peterson & Quammen 1982, Kamermans & Huitema 1994,
  Coen & Heck 1991 - NOT obtained.
- Abra nitida (the Mareld bivalve) siphon >0.5 cm above surface, active 25 % of time at 10 C
  (Gremare et al. 2004, earlier note [F]).

1d. Polychaete tails (intertidal analogue, the only quantified case found)
- Beukema & de Vlas 1979, NJSR 13:331-353 [F, VLIZ OCR, data/beukema_devlas1979_njsr/]:
  lugworm tail ends eaten by flatfish (mainly plaice), Balgzand 1976: 0.3-3.0, mean 1.32
  g AFDW m-2 yr-1 vs mean biomass 5.1 g m-2: "elimination of lugworm biomass by predation on tail
  ends amounted to 26% of the mean annual biomass (24% by plaice plus 2% by flounder)". In 1973
  (Kuipers 1977) 36 %. "Therefore, 30%, or 0.39 as an instantaneous rate, will be a fair estimate
  of lugworm biomass elimination in the form of regenerating tail ends." Adult mortality 0.26 +
  tail cropping 0.39 = 0.65 total; somatic P/B ~0.6-0.7. Consumption was "roughly proportional to
  lugworm biomass at the various sampling places".
  -> cropping removed ~60 % of somatic production (0.39/0.65, J) without killing.
- Bergman, van der Veer & Karczmarski 1988, NJSR 22:83-90 [A, marineinfo 15830]: lab
  amputation of lugworm tail tips: "The loss of body weight by amputation was not compensated for
  by an increased growth. Within a wide range of amputation frequencies, total growth (body growth
  + amount of tail tip amputated) and reproduction of the lugworm were not affected." "Only at the
  highest frequency of amputation (once a week) did total growth decrease ..., resulting even in a
  loss of body weight"; mortality was higher in that group.
- Cirratulid palps roam the surface (MarLIN, earlier note); no palp regrowth rate found.

1e. Share of fish diet that is cropped parts
- de Vlas 1979 NJSR 13:117-153 (plaice/flounder, Wadden Sea): ~1/3 of intake as siphons, tail
  ends, palps, heads - this figure is still only [S] (earlier note, snippet of de Vlas 1981
  chapter "On cropping and being cropped", in Jones & Wolff eds, Feeding and Survival Strategies
  of Estuarine Organisms, Plenum, pp 173-177). The 1979 paper itself was NOT obtained.
- Poiesz et al. 2019 J Sea Res 144:39 [F, NIOZ postprint data/vliz_321200/]: cites the "late
  summer decrease in stomach content biomass in 0-group plaice ... especially of regenerating body
  parts of macrobenthic prey (Kuipers, 1977; de Vlas, 1979 ...)". No share given.
- Mattson 1992 (haddock on Amphiura arms) NOT obtained; the Skagerrak cod stomach report of 1991
  (Dorman et al., ICES CM 1992/G:70, [F] data/vliz_ocrd_274761/) lists Amphiura and Ophiura in cod
  stomachs at low weight but does not separate arms from whole animals.

1f. Is cropping sublethal?
- Lugworm: yes up to near-weekly amputation (Bergman 1988 [A]); Amphiura: 96 % of individuals
  carry regenerating arms (Duineveld & Van Noort [A]) and "dab will have only little if any effect
  on the population" [A]. Bivalves: sublethal, but cropping lowers body weight and burial depth,
  raising later whole-animal predation risk (Zwarts 1994 [A]; de Goeij 2001 [S]); Meyer & Byers
  (SICB abstract, Protothaca) [S]: cropping top 40 % of siphon -> 33-50 % shallower burial.

1g. Synthesis for Q1 (J)
- Exposed regrowable mass per unit biomass:
  Amphiura: exposed tips 2 arms x 1-2 cm of ~5 x 8 cm arm length; distal parts thin -> ~2-8 %
  of the animal's mass exposed at a time (earlier note J). Standing regenerating tissue 22 %
  (12-30 %) is the upper bound of what has been croppable over the past months.
  Bivalves: 0.6 % of body mass per cm of extended siphon -> ~0.5-2 %.
  Polychaetes: lugworm tail ~ order 10-30 % of mass removable per year; per event unknown.
  Community-weighted (100 m Amphiura 65 %, polychaetes 17 %, bivalves 12 %): ~2-6 % exposed and
  regrowable at any time; 300 m (polychaetes 44 %, bivalves 44 %): ~1-5 % (J, wide).
- Regrowth time scale: arm tip 60-200 d (central ~100-120 d); lugworm tails tolerate amputation up to
  every ~2 weeks with full compensation of total growth (Bergman 1988) -> regrowth of order weeks.
- Flux scale: cropping flux measured 0.06 B/yr (Amphiura, all predators) to 0.39 B/yr (lugworm,
  intertidal flatfish). Neither is a 100-300 m fish-specific value.

Q2. ONTOGENETIC DEPTH REFUGE (burial depth vs size; time to grow out of reach)
======================================================================================

2a. Amphiura filiformis (100 m dominant)
- Skold, Josefson & Loo 2001, Mar Biol 139:519-526, doi 10.1007/s002270100600 [A, marineinfo
  260762]: Gullmarsfjord sill 1991. "Maximum settling was recorded in October, with 7,100+-7,400
  (mean+-SD) ind. m-2". "The initial size of the settlers was around 300 um in disk diameter".
  "adult size and sexual maturity (ca. 4 mm disk diameter) are attained around 2 years after
  settlement. The inflexion point in the sigmoidal growth curve occurs ca. 1 year after
  settlement, at an approximate disk diameter of 2 mm." Faster growth after year 1 is attributed
  to the onset of suspension feeding (arms raised = the croppable phase begins).
- Muus 1981 (Oresund) [S via MarLIN]: settlers 0.3 mm take 2 years to reach 1.3 mm disc; "less
  than 5% contributing to the adult population in any given year"; MarLIN adds "perhaps because
  they are unable to bury themselves until they reach a certain size" (a suggestion, not a
  measurement).
- O'Connor et al. 1983 Mar Biol 75:279 [S via search summary]: adults ~95 % of population,
  "annual recruitment was invariably found to be extremely low" (Galway Bay); ~10 % of the 1982
  year class survived the first 8 months [S].
- Josefson 1995, Mar Biol 124:435-442, doi 10.1007/BF00363917 [A, marineinfo 26694]: Kattegat,
  VB K = 0.54 /yr; "the main part of disc growth occurs within the first 5 to 7 yr".
- Adult disc depth 4-6 cm (Buchanan 1964 [S]); Rosenberg et al. 1997 [F]: "buried in the
  sediment with its disk at about 4 cm depth". No measured depth of juvenile discs was found.
=> J: an Amphiura recruit lives at or near the surface for months to ~1-2 yr (disc < 1-2 mm),
   then joins the buried adult stock. Time to grow out of reach (to ~2-4 mm disc and burial at
   several cm): ~1-2 yr (Skold 2001) to >2 yr (Muus 1981). Recruit biomass is tiny (0.3 mm discs),
   so this juvenile window matters for numbers, not for biomass.

2b. Small bivalves (300 m dominants: Abra nitida, Thyasira, Nucula)
- Dauwe et al. 1998 (Skagerrak 280 m) [F, earlier note]: Abra nitida and Thyasira flexuosa
  confined to 0-2 cm; Nucula peaks at 5-10 cm. Zanzerl & Dufour 2017 [F, earlier note]:
  thyasirids burrow 12.2-64.2 mm with "no size or taxon effect".
  => J: at 300 m the two main bivalve groups do NOT gain a depth refuge with size (Abra stays
  shallow at all sizes; thyasirid depth is size-independent). Their refuge is behavioural
  (siphon/foot retraction, short activity time) and small size, not depth.
- Zwarts & Wanink 1993, NJSR 31:441-476 [F, data/zwarts_wanink1993_njsr/ VLIZ OCR]: intertidal
  analogue (Macoma, Scrobicularia, Mya, Cerastoderma, Arenicola, Nereis): "In all six species,
  burying depth increases with size." Seasonal: Mya and Cerastoderma no seasonal change; "The
  other species lived close to the surface in June and burrowed deeply in winter"; Macoma (10-20
  mm) at "only 3-4 cm in the winters of 1982/83 and 1984/85, but at depths of 6-7 cm in the
  winters of 1979/80, 1980/81 and 1986/87"; depth "not regulated by changes in temperature" (day
  length suggested). "the accessible fraction may even vary between 0 and 100%" [F].
  Condition: "the condition of shallow bivalves is about half of those that live more deeply".
- Zwarts & Wanink 1989 Mar Biol 100:227 [A via search summary]: siphon size sets burial depth;
  suspension feeders show little seasonal change in depth, deposit feeders a large one.
- Beukema & de Vlas 1989 MEPS 52:193-200 [F, data/beukema_devlas1989_meps52/]: Macoma spat
  (up to 1 cm) drift by byssus threads in winter tidal currents; "nearly all M. balthica arrive in
  this area in winter by secondary settlement after tidal current transport"; numerous only near
  the tidal inlet; "Further away (more than 10 to 15 km), young M. balthica arrived in low numbers
  only and were smaller." Primary settlement >half a year earlier on high flats.

2c. Polychaetes
- Dauwe 1998 [F, earlier note]: polychaetes 70-80 % of biomass at 10-20 cm at SK (280 m).
  Josefson 1990 MEPS 66:117 [F]: Heteromastus filiformis, "a species which due to its deep
  burrowing habits is likely to be less affected by predation (e.g. Shaffer 1983)".
  No size-depth relation for subtidal polychaetes was found (Zwarts & Wanink 1993 give one only
  for intertidal Nereis and Arenicola: burrow depth increases with worm length).

2d. Seasonal burial changes at depth: NOT found for Skagerrak/Kattegat infauna. Gremare et al.
  2004 [F, earlier note] show Abra nitida activity 25 % (10 C) to 55 % (15 C) of time, i.e.
  exposure, not depth, varies with temperature.

Q3. REACH OF THE FISH (sediment depth from which haddock, cod, flatfish take prey)
======================================================================================
- NO measurement found of haddock digging depth, nor of cod, dab, plaice, long rough dab or
  witch feeding depth in mud. Searches (haddock pit digging, flatfish excavation depth, Hinz 2005
  thesis - HTML block, Mattson 1992 - not found) gave nothing quantitative.
- de Groot (c. 1970), "Digestive system and sensorial factors in relation to the feeding
  behaviour of flatfish" [F, data/vliz_ocrd_268389/, VLIZ OCR; journal/year not printed in the
  OCR]: "Plaice, flounder and dab are day feeders"; dab "more or less pure crustacea feeders",
  "The dab is the most active feeder, a good shrimp hunter. The plaice, on the other hand, feeds
  chiefly on animals that move slowly or hardly at all, as e.g. Pectinaria." (visual, surface
  hunting; no digging depth). de Groot 1971 NJSR 5:121-196 not opened.
- Indirect evidence (J): (i) fish in Wadden/Loch Ewe studies crop siphon and tail TIPS rather
  than dig the animals out (de Vlas; Trevallion 1971 [S via Saward et al. 1975 Mar Biol 29:351
  search summary: "T. tenuis siphons are an important food for juvenile plaice, which feed by
  grazing the siphon tips; these subsequently regenerate"]); (ii) Amphiura is cropped arm by arm
  (Duineveld & Van Noort: 96 % of animals regenerating, few whole-animal kills) - a fish that could
  reach the 4-6 cm disc would take discs; (iii) cropping lowers prey burial depth and THEN makes
  prey accessible to probing birds (Zwarts 1994; de Goeij 2001), implying fish reach only the
  surface layer. Waders: sanderling 60 % of prey from the upper 1 cm, none below 2.7 cm;
  oystercatcher at high prey density took prey in the upper 3 cm (Zwarts & Wanink 1993 [F]).
- Suggested reach for the model (J, not measured): flatfish and cod 0-1 cm plus exposed parts;
  haddock 0-2 cm (up to ~5 cm if it digs, unmeasured). Same as the earlier note's 0-1/0-2/0-5 cm.
- Hypoxia breaks the refuge: Pihl 1994 CJFAS 51:321 [A, CrossRef]: "Several infaunal species
  increased in dietary importance during recent hypoxia" (SE Kattegat, cod, whiting, plaice, dab,
  long rough dab). Pihl et al. 1992 Mar Biol 112:349 [snippet only]: "increased availability of
  benthos to resident predators" under hypoxia (York River). Not relevant to the normoxic
  Mareld area except as evidence that depth/burial normally limits fish access.

Q4. RECOLONISATION OF AN EMPTIED PATCH AT DEPTH
======================================================================================

4a. Gullmarsfjord 1997-2000 (75-118 m mud, the best depth match)
- Rosenberg, Agrenius, Hellman, Nilsson & Norling 2002, MEPS 234:43-53, doi 10.3354/meps234043
  [F, data/rosenberg2002_meps234/]. Fauna "eliminated at depths below about 100 m and severely
  reduced between 80 and 100 m" in 1997; re-oxygenated spring 1998; sampled 9 times to Apr 2000.
  - First colonist Capitella capitata: 960-1300 ind m-2 at 105-118 m in April 1998, 0 a year
    later (Table 2). Then Echinocardium cordatum/flavescens, Thyasira sarsi/equalis, Nereimyra.
  - Biomass: "At the other stations, the recovery in biomass was minor during 1998, but increased
    after this, particularly at Stn 85." (numbers only in Fig. 4; not digitised).
  - Community: Stns 85 and 95 "returned to SS III" (climax) after ~2 yr; at 105 and 118 m "the
    community structure ... approached but did not reach the 'climax' stage" after 2 yr.
  - Amphiura filiformis (Table 2, ind m-2, Apr 1998/1999/2000): 75 m reference 107/187/240;
    85, 95, 105 m 0/0/0; 118 m 3/3/3. BUT the pre-hypoxia baseline (Nilsson & Rosenberg 2000
    Table 2, June 1997 [F]) already had A. filiformis at only 7 ind m-2 at 85 m and none listed
    at 95-118 m (75 m: 97; 60 m: 323). => J: Amphiura was essentially absent from the defaunated
    depths before the event, so these data are NOT a recolonisation test for Amphiura; at 85 m
    a sparse population (7 m-2) had not returned after 2 yr, which is weak evidence of slow return.
    (Station spacing: "The distance between the most widely separated stations is about 11 km".)
  - Abra nitida reappeared at 95-118 m within 1-2 yr (7-147 ind m-2 by 2000; reference 210-477).
  - Mechanism quote: "Elasticity means rapid recovery, which is promoted by the presence of
    undisturbed communities in the vicinity. Such communities can provide colonising larvae.
    Immigration of adults may also be important for some species, e.g. Amphiura filiformis
    (Rosenberg et al. 1997)."
  - Older cases quoted there [S]: Saltkallefjord after pulp-mill closure 1966: first colonisers
    1969, "it took about 3 yr for the top sediment to recover"; "After 5 to 8 yr ... the faunal
    structure was considered as having returned to pre-pollution levels" (Rosenberg 1976).
    Gullmarsfjord 118 m 1979/80 (Josefson & Widbom 1988): Capitella and Heteromastus colonised,
    "the recovery was halted by a new decline in oxygen". "Recovery is generally more rapid in
    shallow waters than in deeper waters (Diaz & Rosenberg 1995)."
- Josefson & Widbom 1988, Mar Biol 100:31-40, doi 10.1007/BF00392952 [A via search summary]:
  115 m; macrofauna disappeared at 0.21 ml O2/l; "The ensuing recolonization, with an initial
  peak of opportunistic capitellid polychaetes, proceeded slowly"; pre-collapse community not
  restored within ~1.5 yr.
- Nilsson & Rosenberg 2000, MEPS 197:139-149 [F, data/nilsson_rosenberg2000_meps197/]: the
  decline phase; critical O2 ~10 % saturation; no biomass numbers in text.

4b. Adult migration rate (Amphiura) - the horizontal exchange speed
- Rosenberg, Nilsson, Hollertz & Hellman 1997, MEPS 159:121-131, doi 10.3354/meps159121
  [F, data/rosenberg1997_meps159/]. Skagerrak 86 m sediment, 0.22 m2 boxes, 10 C, 63 d.
  "a mean number of 90 and 36 ind. box-1 had migrated into the low density side of the box from
  the high density and moderate density side". Diffusion coefficients: high density "mean 6.136 x
  10-5 m2 h-1", moderate "1.801 x 10-5 m2 h-1". "No significant differences in migration rates were
  observed between fed and starved boxes, indicating that dispersal was primarily
  density-dependent rather than food-dependent." "A. filiformis can move both on the sediment
  surface and within the sediment."
  J arithmetic: D = 6.1e-5 m2/h = 0.54 m2/yr (high) and 0.16 m2/yr (moderate); RMS displacement
  sqrt(4Dt) = 1.5 m (high) / 0.8 m (moderate) per year. Diffusive refill time t ~ L^2/(4D):
  a 10 cm fish pit ~2 d; a 1 m patch ~0.5-1.6 yr; a 10 m patch ~50-160 yr; a 1 km cell: never.
  => adult migration refills only metre-scale holes; km-scale refilling needs larvae.

4c. Defaunated boxes on subtidal sediment (Norway)
- Berge 1990, MEPS 66:103-115 [F, data/berge1990_meps66/]: 0.2 m2 boxes of defaunated sediment.
  Oslofjord, 23 m mud (eutrophic): "restoration of the community took less than 3 mo during a
  spring/summer situation ... dominated by opportunistic species (Polydora spp.)". Raunefjord,
  20 m shell sand: "The time needed to reach 90 % of this equilibrium [species number] was ...
  466 d" in control boxes; immigration 0.27 species d-1 box-1 Jun-Nov, 0.24 Nov-Mar. Small (0.2
  m2) patches: "Immigration rates are reduced when the distance to source communities from where
  larvae and adults can immigrate is increased". (Species number, not biomass.)

4d. Patch-size dependence and mechanisms (mostly intertidal)
- Gunther 1992, NJSR 30:45-56 [F, data/gunther1992_njsr/ VLIZ OCR]: "Scales of dispersal vary
  with size and age of the animals involved: (i) large-scale: planktonic larvae, (ii) mesoscale:
  postlarvae and permanent meiofauna, and (iii) small-scale: adult macrofauna." "Settlement of
  planktonic larvae predominates if the disturbed areas are large. In smaller affected plots,
  postlarval transport as well as immigration of adults begin to play a more significant role."
  Small pits (predator feeding pits): "Control levels are reached within several days (Bell &
  Devlin 1983; Frid 1989)" [S]. Postlarval transport distance of Arenicola "ranges around 1 km";
  meiofauna up to 10 km by tidal currents. Builds on Smith & Brumsickle 1989 (L&O; deep-basin
  trays) [S].
- Dernie, Kaiser & Warwick 2003, J Anim Ecol 72:1043-1056, doi 10.1046/j.1365-2656.2003.00775.x
  [A via search summaries; publisher 403]: intertidal plots; "Clean sand communities had the most
  rapid recovery rate following disturbance, whereas communities from muddy sand habitats had the
  slowest physical and biological recovery rates"; infill rate predicts recovery of numbers. Dernie
  PhD (Bangor 2003) [A]: sandflat, "recovery from the lower intensity treatment was complete after
  63 days", higher intensity "at least twice as long". Day counts for mud NOT obtained.
- Tuck et al. 1998, MEPS 162:227-242 [F abstract, data/tuck1998_meps162/]: unfished Scottish sea
  loch, fine mud, 18 mo experimental trawling then 18 mo recovery: "some differences between
  treatment and reference sites still apparent after 18 mo of recovery".
- Trawl meta-analyses (earlier note, not redone): Hiddink et al. 2017 r = 0.82 /yr (3.6 yr from
  0.5K to 0.95K); Pitcher et al. 2022 mud r 0.68 /yr.

4e. Synthesis for Q4 (J)
- Time to recover biomass after full defaunation of a large (km-scale) patch at 80-120 m mud:
  opportunists within months; total biomass "minor" in year 1, rising in year 2; community not
  fully back at 105-118 m after 2 yr; Amphiura absent after 2 yr. Central ~3 yr (2-5 yr), up to
  5-8 yr after heavy enrichment (Saltkallefjord). Consistent with r 0.7-0.8 /yr meta-analyses.
- Mechanism: larval settlement (km scale) for opportunists, polychaetes, Abra, Thyasira.
  Amphiura: no direct recolonisation test at depth was found (Gullmarsfjord deep stations lacked
  it before the event); its known routes are rare successful recruitment (<5 % of settlers per
  yr) plus slow adult migration (~1 m/yr), so refilling of a large Amphiura patch is expected to
  take several years (J, not measured).
- Spatial scale: adults refill metre-scale holes in days (pits) to ~1 yr (1 m); postlarvae
  ~1 km (intertidal); larvae >10 km. A 1 km model cell is refilled only via larvae/growth.

Q5. REFUGE SATURATION - space or food limited? Does exchange depend on density?
======================================================================================
- Rosenberg et al. 1997 MEPS 159:121 [F]: migration of Amphiura is density-dependent and
  food-independent (quotes in 4b). "it has been suggested that the benthic community examined in
  the present study is limited by space rather than by food (Rosenberg 1995). This assumption was
  made because several populations, among them Amphiura filiformis, had extremely large densities
  and biomasses." "In our experiments, A. filiformis was fed daily with food in excess. Despite
  this, they migrated from the crowded treatments to sediments where abundance was smaller;
  numbers and biomasses became similar." At ~3000 ind m-2 "the area of sediment at about 3 to 4
  cm depth covered by disks of A. filiformis can be estimated as 22 % (Rosenberg 1995)".
  => the deep (disc) layer has a finite area; crowding drives lateral spreading (J).
- Skold & Gunnarsson 1996 MEPS 142:203 [F]: "The difference between sites also suggests
  negatively density dependent growth indicative of space or food competition in the densely
  populated offshore sediment" (offshore = higher abundance and biomass; A. filiformis arm
  regeneration rose with food only in the sparser coastal sediment).
- Josefson 1995 Mar Biol 124:435 [A]: "Size specific growth in oral width was density
  independent despite high densities of A. filiformis (> 3000 ind m-2) and high total benthic
  biomass (up to 1000 g wet wt m-2)"; growth weakly correlated with sediment C and N.
- Josefson 1990 MEPS 66:117-130 [F, data/josefson1990_meps66/]: 14 stations below the halocline,
  1972-1988, >170 km: "Total biomasses showed linear increases by a median factor of 1.8"; the
  increase "primarily due to echinoderms, notably the amphiuroid Amphiura filiformis, and
  polychaetes"; "the present results are in accordance with hypotheses that soft-sediment infauna
  in the area is food limited". He rejects predator release: "There are no data showing a general
  decrease in stocks of demersal fish or other potential predators."
- Skold et al. 1994 / O'Connor 1986 (above): arm regeneration draws on the same energy as
  growth and gonads (O'Connor: 16 % of energy; Skold & Gunnarsson: regenerated arm length
  correlates with oocyte size, r = 0.53) => cropping costs are food-limited (J).
- Olafsson, Peterson & Ambrose 1994 (OMBAR 32:65-109, recruitment limitation and adult-larval
  interactions) - only the abstract framing was seen [A]; no numbers.
=> J: the evidence is split. Food limits growth and the large-scale biomass level (Josefson
   1990); crowding (space at the disc layer) drives Amphiura dispersal at >2000 ind m-2 (Rosenberg
   1997), with density-independent growth in the field (Josefson 1995). No study measured a
   "capacity" of the deep layer itself. For a model this supports density dependence in the
   whole-stock K (food), not a separate saturating refuge compartment.

Q6. HOW SUBLETHAL CROPPING HAS BEEN REPRESENTED; IS CROPPED TISSUE COUNTED AS PRODUCTION?
======================================================================================
- Production budgets COUNT it. de Vlas 1979 NJSR 13:362-393 title: "Secondary production by tail
  regeneration in a tidal flat population of lugworms (Arenicola marina), cropped by flatfish";
  de Vlas 1985 NJSR 19:147-164: "Secondary production by siphon regeneration in a tidal flat
  population of Macoma balthica" (titles [F via CrossRef/marineinfo]; contents not obtained).
  Beukema & de Vlas 1979 [F] add tail-end removal (0.39 /yr) to adult mortality (0.26) to get a
  total elimination 0.65 = somatic production at steady state (P/B ~0.6-0.7).
  Skold et al. 1994 [F, earlier note] count regeneration as 13.3 % of Amphiura production.
- Bergman et al. 1988 NJSR 22:83 [A]: no compensatory growth; "total growth (body growth + amount
  of tail tip amputated)" is constant over a wide range of cropping frequencies, so cropping is a
  production flux taken out of body growth, not an extra production (J: in a model, cropped mass
  should be taken from the prey's growth/biomass budget at the normal production efficiency, not
  regenerated for free); breaks down at weekly amputation.
- Polychaete palps regrow faster than intact palps grow: Matthews & Hentschel 2012, Mar Biol
  159:1039-1048, doi 10.1007/s00227-012-1884-y [A, marineinfo 255592]: Polydora cornuta,
  Tijuana Estuary, July: "removed palps regenerated to half the initial length of unmanipulated
  palps" in 3 d, complete in 6 d; "The regeneration rate of removed palps was significantly faster
  than the growth rate of undamaged palps". Worms with both palps removed grew slower.
  Hentschel & Harper 2006, Mar Biol 149:1175-1183, doi 10.1007/s00227-006-0274-8 [A, marineinfo
  259998]: lab flumes; "During the 3-day experiments, damaged palps fully regenerated".
- Behavioural/indirect: Peterson & Skilleter 1994, Oecologia 100:256-267 and 268-278 [A via
  search summary]: juvenile demersal fish crop exposed Macoma siphons and "induce reduction in
  deposit-feeding activity"; cropping interacts with intraspecific competition. Lin & Hines 1994
  Oikos 69:28 [S]: cropping -> shorter siphon -> shallower burial -> more lethal predation.
  Kamermans & Huitema 1994 (shrimp browsing on siphon tips inhibits Macoma feeding and growth)
  NOT obtained.
- Loch Ewe food chain (Edwards & Steele 1968; Steele 1974; Trevallion 1971): siphon grazing by
  0-group plaice with regeneration is the classic case [S via Saward et al. 1975 search summary];
  no model equations obtained.
- No dynamic population model with an explicit "browsing/cropping" term for subtidal infauna was
  found. Mouritsen & Poulin 2003 (JMBA, parasites; cites de Vlas 1981) [S]: partial predation
  "often affects the majority of individuals in a prey population".
=> J: literature treats cropping as (i) part of prey production (regeneration production) and
   (ii) a predator food source at roughly the prey's production timescale. Representation options:
   a regrowable "exposed" pool that is refilled from the prey's own growth (not free mass).

Q7. INDEPENDENT CHECKS - fish share of infauna production; local depletion by fish
======================================================================================
CORRECTION TO THE BRIEF: the "~4 % of infauna production but 70-80 % of crab/Nephrops production"
pair does NOT come from Heath 2005. It comes from the Mackinson & Daskalov 2007 North Sea EwE
(1991 model, EcoBase 457) as computed in research_notes/"Bentos och torskfiskar"/
evidence_C_diets_and_consumption.md [F, computed there]: infaunal macrobenthos 4 % (fish eat
5.62 of P = 136 g WW m-2 yr-1), small infauna (polychaetes) 8 %, large crabs 82 %, Nephrops 71 %,
shrimp 30 %; all benthos 5.6 %; gadoids alone 0.7 %. Heath 2005 (ICES JMS 62:847-868, Table 4,
[F there]) gives fish consumption as 72 / 53 / 33 % (decadal means 1970s/80s/90s) of the
production of MACROBENTHOS CARNIVORES only ("between 50% and 60% ... during the 1970s, but had
declined to 15-20% by the late 1990s" in the text) and tabulates no production for deposit/filter
feeders, so no infauna fraction exists in Heath 2005.

Additional checks found in this round:
- Dauvin 1988, Cah Biol Mar 29:445-467 [F, data/vliz_ocrd_286707/, French]: Bay of Morlaix fine
  sand (western Channel). Fish consumption "entre 1,1 et 5,5 g de poids frais de macrofaune par an
  et par m2" vs "La production macrobenthique 1980-1981 s'elevait a 85 g de poids frais par an et
  par m2 ... dont 45 g produits par les especes utilisees comme proies". "La consommation par les
  Poissons apparait par consequent tres faible par rapport a la production macrobenthique".
  -> 1.3-6.5 % of total macrobenthic production, 2.4-12 % of prey-species production (J).
- Cramer & Daan 1986 (earlier note [F]): cod + haddock 0.5-0.8 g C m-2 yr-1 = "10-25% of the total
  benthos production" against Steele's 2-5 g C (old, coarse production estimate).
- Pihl 1994 CJFAS 51:321-336 [A]: diet shifts with hypoxia; abstract gives no consumption vs
  production share. Full text NOT obtained.
- Greenstreet et al. 1997 ICES JMS 54:243 [A, earlier note]: production "is sufficient to supply
  the food requirements of ... benthivorous demersal fish". Full text NOT obtained.
- Josefson 1990 [F]: 1.8x biomass rise 1972-88 explained by food (organic enrichment), not by
  predator release; deep-burrowing Heteromastus increased.
- Exclusion experiments: Arntz 1977 (Kiel Bay 20 m, 9 cages of 1 m2, 1972-74) [A, marineinfo
  25760]: "Towards the end of the experiment however the fauna was no richer inside than outside
  the cages", explained by cage artefacts (starfish, crabs, gobies inside; H2S). Hall et al. 1993 J
  Anim Ecol 62:59, doi 10.2307/5482 [S, search summary]: crab (Cancer pagurus) exclusion from 2 x 2
  m subtidal plots, west Scotland, 12 mo: no community-level effect despite high power.
  Frid & James 1988 NJSR 22:307 [A] (salt marsh, 2 yr): epibenthic predator exclusion had "no
  significant effect on the infaunal deposit feeders", attributed "to their horizontal mobility
  within the sediment". Reise 1985 (19th EMBS, tidal flats review) [A]: wide-mesh (>=1 cm)
  exclosures that keep out fish: "only large individuals of the macrofauna gain in abundance
  relative to unprotected controls"; narrow mesh (excluding juvenile fish, shrimp, small crabs):
  "most macrofaunal species attain very high population densities (and biomass) because their
  early juveniles survive".
- No subtidal mud (>50 m) fish-exclusion experiment showing local infauna depletion by fish was
  found.
=> J: across the North Sea EwE (4-8 %), Morlaix (1-7 %) and the Kattegat-Skagerrak biomass trend,
   fish take a small share of infauna production; large shares only for mobile epibenthic
   crustaceans. Fish-driven local depletion of infauna is not demonstrated in subtidal mud; where
   fish matter (intertidal) it is through juvenile survival, not adult stock.
