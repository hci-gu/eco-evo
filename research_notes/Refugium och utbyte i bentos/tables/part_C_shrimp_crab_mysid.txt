PART C - Refuge and exposure of mobile epifauna: northern shrimp, small shrimps, crabs, squat lobsters,
hermit crabs, mysids
=====================================================================================================

Scope: how much of the day each taxon is buried / hidden / off the bottom, the fraction exposed to
benthivorous fish on the bottom, day/night catch ratios, and evidence on refuge saturation.
Model context (from the task): current accessible fractions shrimp 0.7, crabs 0.6, hermits 0.8,
Munida 0.3, small shrimps 0.6, mysids 0.6. Site: Skagerrak, 100-300 m mud.

Tags: [F] full text read by me, [A] abstract only, [S] secondary / search snippet, J = my judgement or
derivation. Where a full text I read reports someone else's result, the tag is [F] for the sentence I read
and the original is named as "cited". Files: data/<source>/ ; scripts in scripts/.

Not repeated (already in "Bottensamhallets epifauna"/evidence_A1, evidence_B): Frechette et al. 1983 ratio
6.33 day/night; females migrate less; Skagerrak shrimp survey (Jan-Feb) no sun-height effect;
L. holsatus buries by day (Reiss 2006); suprabenthos 76-97 % in 0-50 cm by day (Cunha 1997).

Ratio naming in part_C_rows.csv: Q3 shrimp rows give day/night ratios (day_night_catch_ratio), Q4 rows
night/day ratios (night_day_catch_ratio); the unit column states the direction.

Exposure convention used below (J): a bottom-trawl catch ratio measures the share of the population inside
the trawl opening (a few m to ~11 m above the bottom). That is the share a bottom-feeding fish can reach.
Shrimp that have left the bottom are not "safe": they are exposed to pelagic and semi-pelagic feeders
(saithe, blue whiting, herring, mackerel), i.e. to a different model group.

Q3.1 Shumway, Perkins, Schick & Stickney 1985. Synopsis of biological data on the pink shrimp, Pandalus
borealis Kroyer, 1838. NOAA Tech. Rep. NMFS 30 / FAO Fisheries Synopsis No. 144 (NOT 142 as in the task).

  Section 2.22 (all findings below are cited by Shumway, not original):
  - "Diurnal vertical migration of P. borealis has been indicated through reduced trawl catches at night in
    the Barents Sea (Bryazgin 1967), in the Gulf of Alaska (Gaffney 1977a), off Norway (Hjort and Ruud 1938;
    Wollebaek 1903), off west Greenland (Horsted and Smidt 1956), and by others (Blacker 1957; Rathjen and
    Fahlen 1962)."
  - TIMING: "Barr and McBride (1967) found that shrimp moved off the bottom in the early evening and remained
    in the entire water column until early morning (Fig. 8)." -> off bottom ~ dusk to dawn, i.e. roughly the
    length of the night (J). Fig. 8 (Barr 1970a, Kachemak Bay, pots surface-to-bottom, ~91 m) shows the
    sampling periods "arranged in order of increasing length of night"; the figure numbers are not readable in
    the text extraction, so no heights are taken from it.
  - THERMOCLINE LIMIT: "Apollonio and Dunton (1969) confirmed vertical migratory behavior in similar
    experiments in the Gulf of Maine but found the extent of vertical movement limited by a thermocline."
    "Apollonio and Dunton (1969) showed no vertical migration through the thermocline in the Gulf of Maine."
  - SEX: "Ovigerous females do not migrate vertically apparently due to a decreased ability to swim
    (Apollonio and Dunton 1969; Haynes and Wigley 1969; Jones and Parsons 1978)." "Vertical migration at night
    has been shown by Barr (1970a) and Apollonio and Dunton (1969) to separate year classes during seasons
    when females are ovigerous as the females do not rise in the water." Ovigerous season in the Skagerrak is
    roughly autumn-spring (eggs carried ~Oct-Apr; general knowledge, not read here) -> J: in winter a large
    share of the adult biomass (berried females) stays on the bottom, consistent with the Skagerrak Jan-Feb
    survey showing no day/night effect.
  - SIZE (conflicting): "Barr (1970a) found that smaller shrimp migrated further up in the water column off
    Alaska while Carlsson (1979) reported that larger shrimp were participating in the migration to a greater
    extent than smaller shrimp off west Greenland."
  - LIGHT: "Barr (1970a) demonstrated that light affects shrimp in their diel vertical migration in Alaska";
    "Apollonio and Dunton (1969) also demonstrated negative phototaxis of P. borealis adults."
  - FEEDING: "While the species is predominantly a benthic feeder, it is known to feed in the water column
    during diel vertical migrations (Wollebaek 1903; Barr 1970a)."
  - ASSESSMENT BIAS: "Hoydal (1978b) indicated that vertical migration influenced distribution and affected
    catch per unit effort measurements which caused assessments based on bottom trawls to underestimate stock
    size."
  - SCHOOLING: "Pandalus borealis is a schooling species that aggregates in relatively dense patches."
  No number for hours off bottom or share migrating in Shumway.

Q3.5 Barr, L. 1970. Diel vertical migration of Pandalus borealis in Kachemak Bay, Alaska. J. Fish. Res. Board
Can. 27: 669-676. https://doi.org/10.1139/f70-071   [A] (abstract via Crossref; full text closed)
  - "Catches of Pandalus borealis by surface-to-bottom pot fishing in Kachemak Bay, Alaska, showed that this
    species was distributed in large numbers throughout the water column at night and suggested a diel vertical
    migration. The shrimp apparently left the vicinity of the bottom about dusk and returned about dawn; the
    apparent length of time they were off bottom was directly related to the length of the night. Bottom
    trawling clearly showed lower availability of P. borealis on bottom at night than during the day. Catches
    of shrimp by pot fishing and trawling indicated that small P. borealis have a greater tendency to migrate
    vertically than the larger individuals."
  - HOURS OFF BOTTOM (J from Barr): ~ hours of darkness, i.e. at 58 N in the Skagerrak ~6-7 h (midsummer) to
    ~17-18 h (midwinter) for the migrating part of the population. Barr gives no share of the population.
  - Shallow site (Kachemak Bay is a fjord-like bay, tens of m to ~100 m); whole water column used at night.

Q3 SUMMARY (J)
  - Who migrates: males and small / non-ovigerous shrimp; ovigerous females stay on the bottom (Shumway;
    Hudon 1992: 75 % of pelagic P. montagui migrants were males). Size effect conflicting (Barr: small migrate
    more; Carlsson 1979 W Greenland: large more).
  - When: dusk to dawn; time off bottom scales with night length (Barr). Day/night contrast largest in autumn
    (equinox), smallest at the solstices at high latitude (Smidt 1976).
  - How much: bottom-trawl catch at night = 0.3-0.55 of the day/noon catch (ratios 1.8-3.5 Davis Strait, up to
    6.33 Canada; P. montagui males 1.52). 24-h mean on-bottom availability relative to the day maximum: 0.51-0.75
    (W Greenland Oct-Dec), 0.68 (Davis Strait August). Large (fishable, mostly female) shrimp: small effect
    (Baker 2024). Skagerrak survey in Jan-Feb (berried females, long nights): no effect (evidence_A1).
  - How high: P. borealis up to the whole water column in shallow bays (Barr; Kachemak Bay), limited by the
    thermocline (Apollonio & Dunton); P. montagui > 200 m above bottom (Crawford 1992). Jones & Parsons: some
    shrimp above the 11 m trawl opening at all hours.
  - Exposure: on the bottom by day = reachable by benthivores; in the water column at night = exposed to
    pelagic/semi-pelagic fish instead. No study found that measures predation risk on vs off the bottom.
  - Implication for f 0.7 (J): a year-round mean of ~0.65-0.85 is consistent with the evidence (lower in
    autumn, ~1 in winter when females are berried); 0.7 sits at the low end of that.

Q4. SMALL SHRIMPS, CRABS, SQUAT LOBSTERS, HERMIT CRABS, MYSIDS
=====================================================================================================

Depth caveat for all Mediterranean numbers below (J): light at 100-110 m in the clear western Mediterranean is
much higher than at 100 m in the Skagerrak (more turbid, coastal water). The Skagerrak 100-300 m floor is
probably closer, in light, to the Mediterranean upper slope (400 m) than to its shelf (100 m) - so the "slope"
folding factors are likely the more transferable ones. Not measured; a judgement.

  - Atkinson 1974 Mar. Biol. 25: 239-252 (https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00394970) and Rice & Chapman 1971 Mar. Biol.

Q4.8 Zainal, K.A.Y. 1990. Aspects of the biology of the squat lobster, Munida rugosa (Fabricius, 1775). PhD
thesis, University of Glasgow. https://theses.gla.ac.uk/78167/  file: data/zainal1990_thesis/zainal1990.pdf [F]
  Firth of Clyde, 8-115 m; M. sarsi 95-115 m.
  - "It has been noted that M. rugosa occupies the burrows of other species, for example, they have been
    reported from burrows of the red band-fish Cepola rubescens (Atkinson et al., 1977) and those of the Norway
    lobster, Nephrops norvegicus (R.J.A. Atkinson pers. comm.). In each case the burrows had been vacated by
    the original occupants."
  - Divers reported "M. rugosa on open bedrock, beneath stones and rock overhangs (particularly at the
    bedrock/sediment interface), in rock crevices, amongst hydroid and bryozoan 'turf' and on the sediment
    plain. Here they were often adjacent to stones and other cover, but also occurred in burrows."
  - "more animals were caught in creels when they were deployed near to rocks than when they were further out on
    the sediment plain."
  - Lab: "Animals were observed to occupy hollows in the muddy substrata of tanks, but none were observed to
    burrow independently." "Independent burrowing behaviour could not be substantiated".
  - "Observations suggest that those specimens that do occur in crevices and burrows, remain at or near the
    opening (R.J.A. Atkinson, pers. comm.)."
  - Feeding time: "more full stomachs (category 1) were found in the morning catches than in afternoon catches,
    suggesting a nocturnal feeding peak": >50 % full 29 of 71 (morning) vs 8 of 43 (afternoon).

  - REFUGE SATURATION (J): on featureless mud, Munida shelter depends on burrows dug by other species (Nephrops,
    Goneplax, Cepola) and on stones. This is a refuge that can saturate, but no study quantifying vacant-burrow
    availability vs Munida density was found.

  - "In both Lophogaster typicus and Leptomysis gracilis, the entire population seems to leave the bottom at

=====================================================================================================
REFUGE SATURATION - what was found (J summary)
=====================================================================================================
  - Burrowers that dig their own refuge (Goneplax, Alpheus, Nephrops): no limit on refuges implied; Goneplax
    burrows 0.93 /m2 at 17 m (Atkinson, Lundy) - occupancy unknown.
  - Munida: does not dig (Zainal 1990); depends on vacated burrows of Nephrops/Goneplax/Cepola, stones and
    crevices; stays at the opening. Refuge availability could limit it on open mud. No numbers.
  - L. depurator: buries or sits in depressions; at Lundy seen on the surface by day (inactive). Burial in soft
    sediment is not limited by space (J).
  - Processa, Solenocera, Pontophilus: bury in sediment; unlimited substrate (J).
  - Mysids: Lophogaster burrows; others stay in the bottom 0.5 m by day (refuge = proximity to bottom /
    darkness, not a countable shelter).
  - Hermits: portable shell; shell supply is limiting in P. bernhardus (intertidal/shallow, search snippets),
    unquantified, and not a refuge from fish that crush shells.
