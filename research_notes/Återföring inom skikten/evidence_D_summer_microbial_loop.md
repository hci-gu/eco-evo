# D - Summer microbial loop, Kattegat / Skagerrak / North Sea

Literature check for the model problem: surface phytoplankton carbon in summer is
4-7 ug C/l in the model but 32-56 ug C/l at A17. The lumped microzooplankton group
(food threshold ~12 ug C/l, constant mortality 0.05/d, GGE 0.3) dies out from May to
December.

Grades: [F] full text read; [A] abstract only; [S] search snippet only; [N] not found
or no access. Arithmetic marked "(Own calculation)" is mine. Unit identity used
throughout: 1 ng C/ml = 1 ug C/l = 1 mg C/m3.

Already known and not repeated here: Nielsen & Kiorboe 1994, Hansen 1991, Hansen et
al. 1997, Calbet & Landry 2004.

---

## 0. Bottom line for the model

1. **Observed summer pools in the low-nutrient surface layer are far above the model's
   4-7 ug C/l.** In the central Skagerrak in June 1990, with surface nitrate below the
   0.05 uM detection limit, the 0-15 m layer held about 65 ug C/l of autotrophs
   (Bjornsen et al. 1993, Fig. 12). It also held about 10 ug C/l of heterotrophic
   protists (HNANO 4-5, HDino 3.4-3.5, ciliates 0.8-1.4) and 16-23 ug C/l of
   bacteria. A caveat on the 65: their Table 1 chl a of 0.4-0.6 ug/l times their own
   C:chl of 30 gives only 12-18 ug C/l (Own calculation). The 65 is presumably
   microscopy (biovolume) carbon. Both numbers are given in section 2, and this
   report does not resolve the gap. Either way, the surface protist community
   persisted all summer on that prey field.
2. **Kattegat ciliates did not starve in summer.** They grew at near-maximal,
   temperature-limited rates "independent of various measures of food availability"
   (Nielsen & Kiorboe 1994, as cited by Levinsen & Nielsen 2002 [F]). The heterotrophic
   dinoflagellates were the group that was "usually food limited" in summer. They
   dropped to pre-bloom levels after the spring bloom and rose again in late summer.
   Neither group disappeared.
3. **A threshold of 12 ug C/l is not high by lab standards. It sits at the low end of
   the measured values.** Lab thresholds for small ciliates and heterotrophic
   dinoflagellates cluster at 6-20 ug C/l, with a long tail to 75-325 ug C/l
   (Jakobsen & Hansen 1997, Table 4 [F]; Montagnes 1996 [F]). Most dilution
   experiments, by contrast, find no threshold. That tension is real. The ERSEM
   source found here (Vichi et al. 2004 [F]) lists a Z5 "feeding threshold" of
   50 mg C/m3. It is not clear from what I read whether ERSEM uses it as a hard
   cut-off or as a saturating "perception" term.
4. **Starvation tolerance differs by group.** Naked oligotrich ciliates die within 1-3
   days below threshold. Other ciliates last 5-7 days. Heterotrophic dinoflagellates
   last weeks to more than 2 months: 30 d for Gymnodinium, 71 d for Protoperidinium.
   They cut respiration by 25-70% when starved, and some make swarmers. A single
   lumped group with a constant mortality of 0.05/d, and with zero growth below a
   threshold, removes the dinoflagellate "persistence" strategy. Overall, the
   literature points first at the phytoplankton carbon being 5-10x too low, and
   second at the lumped constant-mortality group. It does not point at the threshold
   value itself.
5. **Mixotrophy and phototrophic ciliates matter.** Mesodinium rubrum made up more
   than 50% of Kattegat ciliate biomass in February-April (Levinsen & Nielsen 2002
   [F]), and Laboea strobila occurs there. Elsewhere, plastidic ciliates are typically
   30-90% of summer oligotrichs (Stoecker et al. 2009 review [F]). I found no
   Kattegat/Skagerrak summer mixotroph share [N].
6. **Bacteria are a large carbon sink. Micrograzers are probably the main NH4
   regenerators.** Surface bacterial production was about 15-20% of PP in the June
   Skagerrak (Own calculation from Bjornsen et al. 1993). It was "of a similar
   magnitude to primary production" at stratified stations in May (Kiorboe et al.
   1990), though that estimate was probably inflated. Bjornsen et al. 1993 state that
   "herbivorous micrograzers (viz. ciliates and heterotrophic dinoflagellates) were
   responsible for a substantial excretion of nutrients which had not been processed
   by bacteria".

**Verdict:** 12 ug C/l sits at the low end of the lab range for small ciliates and
heterotrophic dinoflagellates (6-20 ug C/l). It is not high compared with ERSEM III
(50) or the oligotrich range of 10-100. Field dilution studies usually find no
threshold. A threshold of 12 only kills the group because the model's prey pool
(4-7 ug C/l) is below it. With the observed 12-65 ug C/l (section 2) the same
threshold would allow growth.

**For the model:** the measured mortality below threshold is not a constant 0.05/d.
For oligotrichs it is roughly 1-2/d (all dead in 1-3 d). For heterotrophic
dinoflagellates it is much lower: 30-71 d survival implies roughly 0.065-0.15/d (Own
calculation: ln(100)/71 to ln(100)/30, assuming exponential decay to 1% survivors). These dinoflagellates
also cut respiration by 25-70%. A lumped group with a fixed 0.05/d loss and a hard
threshold therefore behaves like neither group. The dinoflagellate-like strategy
(lowered metabolism, slow loss) is the one that carries the population through a
nutrient-poor summer.

**For the model:** plastidic oligotrichs (Strombidium, Laboea) and M. rubra cover
part of their carbon demand by photosynthesis. In summer they can be a large share of
the oligotrichs (up to 90% at Woods Hole). This lowers the prey concentration at which
net growth is zero. A purely heterotrophic lumped group with a 12 ug C/l threshold
misses that subsidy.

**For the model:** in nitrate-depleted summer water, PP runs on regenerated N. The
main regenerators named for the Skagerrak are the herbivorous micrograzers.
Bacteria, with a growth yield of about 0.2-0.3, are a carbon sink. They take a median
of about 30% of NH4 uptake, so they compete with phytoplankton for N more than they
supply it. If microzooplankton dies out in the model, the main summer NH4 recycling
pathway disappears with it. That is consistent with a collapsing summer phytoplankton
pool (4-7 vs 32-56 ug C/l).
