# B. Copepod faecal pellets: export vs retention in the upper ~30 m (shelf seas, Kattegat/Skagerrak/North Sea)

Grading: [F] full text read, [A] abstract only, [S] search snippet / secondary citation only, [N] not found / no access.
"(Own calculation)" = my arithmetic, not in the source.

## 0. Bottom line for the model

- Treating 100 % of copepod egesta as exported below 30 m is not supported as a general rule. Which way the
  evidence points depends on (a) pellet size, i.e. who makes the pellets (Calanus / Pseudocalanus / Temora vs
  Oithona, Acartia, nauplii), (b) how much egesta never becomes a pellet, and (c) season (degradation rate
  0.5 d-1 in late winter up to 2.5 d-1 at the spring bloom).
- Envelope the sources support (summary, partly own synthesis):
  - **Large/medium calanoid pellets** (Calanus, Pseudocalanus, Temora), and spring:
    roughly **50-85 % exported** (Gullmar Fjord 30 m: ~84 %; Disko Bay, Calanus: ~65 %).
  - **Small-copepod or Oithona-dominated summer communities**: **~5-40 % exported**, i.e. 60-97 % retained
    (North Sea Aug 2002: 3-39 % reached 50 m).
  - **All egesta including non-pellet-bound faecal matter** (Acartia: >50 % of egesta not in pellets):
    **>80 % retained in a 15 m mixed layer** (Olesen et al. 2005).
- In a steady-state layer model, export fraction = s/(s + r), with s = v/H (sinking loss rate), v = sinking speed,
  H = layer thickness, r = degradation rate. For H = 30 m: v = 5 m d-1, r = 0.5 d-1 gives 25 % exported;
  v = 25 m d-1, r = 1 d-1 gives 45 %; v = 5 m d-1, r = 3 d-1 gives 5 %. Pellets of 100-200 m d-1 (large
  calanoids, diatom diets) give ~87-93 % at r = 0.5 d-1. (Own calculation for all of these.)
- Nitrogen is recycled preferentially. The Kattegat trap material had C/N 8.6 vs 6.4 for suspended POM, so a
  carbon-based export share overstates the PON exported.

## 1. The tension: Lundsgaard & Olesen 1997 vs strong-retention studies

**Lundsgaard C, Olesen M (1997)** The origin of sedimenting detrital matter in a coastal system. Limnol Oceanogr
42(5):1001-1005. doi:10.4319/lo.1997.42.5.1001. **[A]** (abstract via marineinfo.org/id/publication/145415; Wiley
full text 403, Unpaywall says closed).
- "Total sedimentation and the fraction due to copepod fecal pellets were measured during the growth season
  (March-October 1989) in the southern Kattegat ... sedimentation of detritus made up 52 g C m-2, equal to 82 % of
  the sedimenting matter from the euphotic zone, but fecal pellets (11 g C m-2) constituted only a minor
  fraction. The remaining detrital matter was produced by other heterotrophs than copepods." ... "Copepod
  defecation nearly equaled the sedimentation of fecal pellets, indicating that retention of this matter in the
  pelagic system was insignificant." ... "A considerable fraction (10-24 %) of the carbon flow processed by
  heterotrophic pico-, nano-, and microplankton was converted to detritus that was lost from the mixed system by
  sedimentation."
- Same data set as Olesen & Lundsgaard 1995 (below): traps at the pycnocline of a 29 m deep station (station depth
  from Carstensen et al. 2003 [F]); pycnocline 10-20 m in late spring/summer (Richardson & Christoffersen 1991 [F]).
  So "export" here means below roughly 10-20 m, not below 30 m.
- Discrepancy between the two abstracts of the same 1989 data set, which I cannot resolve without the full texts:
  Olesen & Lundsgaard 1995 say "Faecal pellets contributed on average less than 5 %" of 63 g C m-2, i.e.
  <3.2 g C m-2 (own calculation). Lundsgaard & Olesen 1997 give pellets 11 g C m-2 = 17.5 % of 63 (own calculation).
  Both are reported here; I have not picked one.
- **Unresolved:** how the 1997 "copepod defecation" term was estimated (pellet production rates? literature
  egestion ratios? did it include non-pellet faecal matter?). The full text is needed.

**How the tension resolves (from [F] sources):**
1. **Same author, eight years later.** Olesen, Strake & Andrushaitis 2005 [F] opens: "Only a minor fraction of
   copepod defecation appears to leave the upper water column as fast-sinking fecal pellets in coastal waters." It
   shows that >50 % of Acartia egesta is released as non-pellet-bound particles <10 um. If the 1997 "defecation"
   term counted pellets only, then "defecation ~ pellet sedimentation" (pellets are exported) and ">80 % of total
   faecal matter retained" can both be true. The 1997 statement would then be about intact pellets, not total
   egesta. This reading is my inference, not verified.
2. **Community composition.** Kattegat traps catch pellets of calanoids (Pseudocalanus, Temora, Centropages,
   Acartia). Poulsen & Kiorboe 2006 found 61-97 % retention in an Oithona-dominated community. Gullmar Fjord
   (Vargas et al. 2002 [F], the nearest Skagerrak analogue, traps at 15 and 30 m) found the pellets of the larger
   copepods "were recovered in the traps" (regression slope 0.84, ~16 % recycled) and faecal flux *increased* with
   depth (22 mg C m-2 d-1 at 15 m, 37 at 30 m). Small Oikopleura pellets and Fritillaria houses were presumed
   recycled. González & Smetacek 1994 (as cited in Poulsen & Kiorboe 2006 [F]): "at cyclopoid:Calanus spp.
   ratios > 0.3 the residence time of calanoid fecal pellets increased significantly, and at ratios > 4 almost all
   calanoid pellets were degraded in the water column."
3. **Shallow layer, fast pellets.** With a pycnocline trap at ~15 m, a 5 m d-1 pellet has a residence time of
   ~3 d; a 50-100 m d-1 pellet has hours. Retention falls steeply with sinking speed and rises with layer depth.
4. **Season.** Pellet degradation in Øresund (next to the Kattegat) ran from 0.52 d-1 in late winter to 2.5 d-1 at
   the spring bloom (Poulsen & Iversen 2008 [F]). A March-October trap budget averages over very different regimes.

## 2. Retention / flux-attenuation studies

**Poulsen LK, Kiorboe T (2006)** Vertical flux and degradation rates of copepod fecal pellets in a zooplankton
community dominated by small copepods. MEPS 323:195-204. doi:10.3354/meps323195. **[F]**
(int-res.com/articles/meps2006/323/m323p195.pdf)
- Northern North Sea, 7-14 Aug 2002, water depth 56-58 m, drifting trap array near the amphidromic point; sampling
  at 10-50 m; thermocline at 15-30 m depth "separating ~18 °C surface" water from the bottom layer; community
  dominated by *Oithona similis*.
- "Only 3 to 39 % of the fecal pellets produced in the upper 50 m of the water column were collected by sediment
  traps at 50 m, corresponding to specific degradation rates of fecal pellets of 1 to 3 d-1."
- Table 2, six trap deployments (column order read from the pdftotext dump; checked by recomputing residence time
  = abundance/production, own calculation):
  - flux/production 39, 25, 36, 10, 3, 9 %
  - "sinking velocity" 21, 16, 25, 13, 2, 4 m d-1
  - residence time 0.7, 0.4, 0.5, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8 d
  - specific sinking loss 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 0.3, 0.1, 0.1 d-1
  - total degradation r1 0.9, 1.7, 1.4, 2.8, 2.1, 1.2 d-1
  - degradation by zooplankton >200 um r2 ~0 to 0.002 d-1
- **Caveat:** the sinking velocities are steady-state estimates derived from flux and abundance, not measurements
  of individual pellets.
- Pellet ESD: produced 65 ± 13 um, suspended 63 ± 14 um, in traps 80 ± 18 um, so large pellets are exported
  preferentially. Pellet production 30-174 x 10^4 pellets m-2 d-1 = 2-10 pellets copepod-1 d-1.
- "copepods and other zooplankton organisms > 200 um did not play an important role in the degradation ...
  3 orders of magnitude lower than the total degradation rates". Degradation is attributed to organisms <200 um.
- After a wind event, pellets became smaller and sinking rate and sinking loss dropped "by a factor of 5".
- Bacteria "are generally not believed to be the main degraders of freshly egested fecal pellets in the sea on a
  time-scale of <1 d (= residence time)".
- Precursor: Poulsen & Kiorboe ASLO/TOS 2004 conference abstract (orbit.dtu.dk) [A]: "<5 % of the fecal pellets
  produced in the upper 50 m were lost as flux below 50 m" (10-day North Sea study). The 2006 paper (3-39 %)
  supersedes it.

**Poulsen LK, Kiorboe T (2005)** Coprophagy and coprorhexy in the copepods *Acartia tonsa* and *Temora
longicornis*: clearance rates and feeding behaviour. MEPS 299:217-227. doi:10.3354/meps299217. **[F]**
- Pellet clearance "similar but low on their own pellets (11 to 22 ml female-1 d-1)"; clearance decreases with
  relative pellet size; phytoplankton raises pellet clearance.
- "fecal pellets were mainly degraded by fragmentation during rejection and only a small fraction was actually
  ingested (5 %)"; ~95 % of pellet particles caught were rejected. The main effect is coprorhexy, making smaller,
  slower-sinking particles.

**Iversen MH, Poulsen LK (2007)** Coprorhexy, coprophagy, and coprochaly in the copepods *Calanus helgolandicus*,
*Pseudocalanus elongatus*, and *Oithona similis*. MEPS 350:79-89. doi:10.3354/meps07095. **[F]**
- "Actual ingestion of captured pellets was rare (< 37 % for C. helgolandicus and < 24 % for P. elongatus)";
  O. similis showed no pellet capture in their set-up. Coprorhexy turns pellets into particles "that can then be
  degraded by other organisms such as bacteria and protozooplankton".
- Cites Ploug et al. (in press, 2008) for sinking velocities of "28 ± 3 m d-1" for similar-sized pellets.

**Poulsen LK, Iversen MH (2008)** Degradation of copepod fecal pellets: key role of protozooplankton. MEPS
367:1-13. doi:10.3354/meps07611. **[F]** (open access)
- Øresund (Denmark/Sweden), water from the fluorescence maximum, about every second month, Jul 2004 to Jul 2005.
- Total degradation "maximum ... during the spring bloom (2.5 ± 0.49 d-1) and minimum (0.52 ± 0.14 d-1) during
  late winter. Total pellet removal rate ranged from 22 % d-1 (July 2005) to 87 % d-1 (May)." In the discussion,
  corrected r = 0.42-2.53 d-1.
- Protozooplankton 20-100 um (dinoflagellates, ciliates) contributed "15 to 53 % of the total degradation rate";
  free-living bacteria had no significant effect; pellet-associated (culture) bacteria up to 59 %; mesozooplankton
  not significant. Copepods act indirectly by grazing the protozoan degraders (July: many copepods, low
  degradation).
- Proposes replacing the "coprophagous filter" with a "protozoan filter".

**Olesen M, Strake S, Andrushaitis A (2005)** Egestion of non-pellet-bound fecal material from the copepod
*Acartia tonsa*: implication for vertical flux and degradation. MEPS 293:131-142. doi:10.3354/meps293131. **[F]**
- "> 50 % of the defecation was released as dispersed small (<10 um) non-pellet-bound particles."
- "total egestion comprised 18 % of the ingestion of Rhodomonas and 27 % of the Skeletonema ingestion."
- Pellet sinking "ca. 5 m d-1"; degradation "ca. 0.5 d-1 (18 °C) in the absence of copepods", about 50 % higher
  with copepods present (not significant).
- Discussion (worked example): "Considering a mixed layer of 15 m, a sinking velocity of 5 m d-1 corresponds to a
  33 % loss of the fecal pellets across the pycnocline per day. When a degradation rate of around 50 % per day is
  also considered, then 40 % of fecal pellets produced will leave the mixed layer ... (60 %) will be degraded
  within the mixed layer ... If this [non-pellet] material comprises two thirds of the total egestion, up to 90 %
  of the fecal material from copepods such as Acartia will be decomposed in the mixed layer."
- Abstract: "60 % of the fecal pellets ... recycled within a 15 m deep mixed layer and ... > 80 % of the total
  fecal matter can be expected to be retained."
- Cites Viitasalo et al. 1999 (Baltic): "> 99 % of the fecal matter was recycled in the upper water column".

**Vargas CA, Tönnesson K, Sell A, ... Tiselius P (2002)** Importance of copepods versus appendicularians in
vertical carbon fluxes in a Swedish fjord. MEPS 241:125-138. doi:10.3354/meps241125. **[F]**
- Gullmar Fjord (Skagerrak coast), Oct 1999 and Mar 2000, 24 h traps at 15 and 30 m, production integrated over
  0-30 m.
- "Our data for larger copepods, however, show that most of the produced pellets were recovered in the traps. A
  regression of pellets recovered in the traps vs pellets produced (Fig. 7) shows that recycling was significant
  ... even when only approximately 16 % was recycled (slope: 0.84)."
- Faecal flux 22 (15 m) vs 37 (30 m) mg C m-2 d-1; Pseudocalanus 8.3 mg C m-2 d-1 the largest single contributor
  (Oct). In March, Pseudocalanus 2.1 and Temora 2.5 mg C m-2 d-1 at 30 m.
- October PP 456 mg C m-2 d-1. Copepod pellets below 30 m = "6.0 % PP", appendicularian pellets 0.8 %, houses
  3.4 %, all zooplankton-mediated flux 11.4 % of PP, ">85 % of the primary production was available to other
  pelagic processes". March: faecal flux 1.4-5.5 % of PP, ungrazed cell flux 63-189 % of PP (not bloom
  conditions).
- Oikopleura houses: 18,900 produced vs 5800 reaching the traps, m-2 d-1 (~70 % d-1 loss).

**Juul-Pedersen T et al. (2006)** Sedimentation following the spring bloom in Disko Bay, West Greenland, with
special emphasis on the role of copepods. MEPS 314:239-255. doi:10.3354/meps314239. **[A]**
- June 2001, Calanus spp. up to 88 % of copepod biomass. POC flux from the euphotic zone 628 mg C m-2 d-1, faecal
  pellets 29 % of it. "on average, 35 % of the copepod faecal pellet production was retained within the euphotic
  zone." POC:PON 8.1 suspended vs 8.0 in traps, "low retention efficiency of nitrogen just after the spring bloom".

**Kiorboe T (2003)** High turnover rates of copepod fecal pellets due to *Noctiluca scintillans* grazing. MEPS
258:181-188. doi:10.3354/meps258181. **[A]** (Brazil upwelling plume, not a shelf analogue for the Skagerrak, but it
gives the mechanism)
- "Less than half (20 to 45 %) of the pellets produced in the overlying water column reached sediment traps
  positioned at 30 to 60 m depth"; sinking losses 0.3-2.9 d-1; remineralisation 0.3-13 d-1, linear in Noctiluca
  abundance (clearance ~0.6 l cell-1 d-1). Noctiluca "typical abundances of about 10^6 cells m-2 in temperate seas
  during spring, summer and autumn".

**González HE, Smetacek V (1994)** The possible role of the cyclopoid copepod *Oithona* in retarding vertical flux of
zooplankton faecal material. MEPS 113:233-246. **[F]** (abstract and front matter read)
- Svalbard shelf. Oithona removed calanoid faecal material in experiments; "About 20 to 30 % of the calculated
  daily carbon requirements of Oithona could have been met from faecal matter"; proposes the "coprophagous
  filter".
- Later contested: Reigstad et al. 2005 found no Oithona feeding on Calanus pellets in rotating containers (via
  Turner 2015 [F]); Poulsen & Kiorboe 2006 and Iversen & Poulsen 2007 (above).

**Turner JT (2015)** Zooplankton fecal pellets, marine snow, phytodetritus and the ocean's biological pump. Prog
Oceanogr 130:205-248. doi:10.1016/j.pocean.2014.08.005. **[F]** (us-ocb.org PDF)
- "Such retention of fecal pellets may range from 30 % to 98 % (Svensen et al., 2012)."
- Recognisable pellets' share of POC flux "highly variable (<1->100 % - Table 2), with most values <40 %".
- Table 2 (all [S] for the underlying papers):
  - Barents Sea, upper 200 m: 8-40 % (Wexels Riser 2002), 1-37 % (2007), 13-27 % (2008)
  - Iberian shelf: >20 % on-shelf, 0.3-6.7 % off-shelf (Wexels Riser 2001)
  - Balsfjord, upper 100 m: 7-75 % (Wexels Riser 2010)
  - Disko Bay: 29 % (Juul-Pedersen 2006)
  - Northwest Pacific: 3-39 % (Wilson 2008)
- Svensen et al. 2012: large *C. finmarchicus* pellets lost 32 % of volume after 72 h with <180 um plankton, 75 %
  after 48 h with *C. finmarchicus* added. This is "too slow to explain observed retention of these large
  fast-sinking fecal pellets in the upper 200 m".

**Turner JT (2002)** Zooplankton fecal pellets, marine snow and sinking phytoplankton blooms. Aquat Microb Ecol
27:57-102. doi:10.3354/ame027057. **[F]**
- Table 2: copepod pellet sinking rates **5-220 m d-1**; euphausiids 16-862; appendicularians 25-166 m d-1.
- Cites Smetacek 1980 (Kiel Bight): calanoid pellets <10 % of detrital POC Apr-Sep, "Calculated residence times

**Viitasalo M, Rosenberg M, Heiskanen A-S, Koski M (1999)** Sedimentation of copepod fecal material in the coastal

Residence in a 30 m layer, by sinking alone, is H/v (own calculation):
- 5 m d-1: 6 d
- 25 m d-1: 1.2 d
- 100 m d-1: 0.3 d
- 200 m d-1: 0.15 d

The fraction that escapes degradation is s/(s + r) with s = v/H (well-mixed steady state). An upper bound for
pellets produced at the surface and sinking without mixing is exp(-r H/v). (Own calculation.)

## 5. Kattegat / Skagerrak / North Sea export of primary production

- **Olesen M, Lundsgaard C (1995)** Seasonal sedimentation of autochthonous material from the euphotic zone of a
  coastal system. Estuar Coast Shelf Sci 41:475-490. **[A]**
  - Southern Kattegat, Mar-Oct 1989, flux across the pycnocline: total C flux 63 g C m-2; viable phytoplankton
    18 %; "Faecal pellets contributed on average less than 5 %"; mainly detritus "estimated mainly to originate
    from the activity of microzooplankton"; daily flux = 14 % of phytoplankton biomass.
  - **Richardson K, Christoffersen A (1991)** MEPS 78:217-227 [F] (same station, 1989): "total annual primary
    production ... ca 290 g C m-2"; ~19 % in the spring bloom, ~30 % in subsurface (pycnocline) populations;
    pycnocline at "ca 10 and 20 m".
  - Pycnocline export in 1989 = 63/290 ≈ **22 % of annual PP** (Mar-Oct flux only; winter excluded). Pellets
    11/290 ≈ 3.8 % of PP using the 1997 figure, or <1.1 % using the 1995 "<5 %". (Own calculation.)
- **Carstensen J, Conley D, Müller-Karulis B (2003)** Spatial and temporal resolution of carbon fluxes in a
  shallow coastal ecosystem, the Kattegat. MEPS 252:35-50. doi:10.3354/meps252035. **[F]**
  - Monitoring-based budget model, 0-10 m layer, 1989-1997: PP 116 g C m-2 yr-1 for the whole region (171 coastal,
    105 open water); "Annual sedimentation was estimated at 55 g C m-2 yr-1, equivalent to 54 % of net primary
    production"; respiration 12 % of PP; winter (Nov-Feb) up to 14 % of annual sedimentation.
  - The model "did not include the sedimentation of faecal pellets from copepods, which in previous studies was
    found to be of minor importance in this context (Lundsgaard & Olesen 1997)".
- **Gullmar Fjord, Oct 1999** (Vargas et al. 2002 [F]): chl a flux below 30 m "never exceeded 4 % d-1, or 11.2 % of
  the PP"; copepod pellets 6.0 % of PP; total zooplankton-mediated 11.4 % of PP.
- **Northern North Sea, Aug 2002** (Poulsen & Kiorboe 2006 [F]): pellet flux at 50 m 5-49 x 10^4 pellets m-2 d-1.
  No PP ratio given.
- **Skagerrak proper (open, deep basin)**: no trap study found in this search **[N]**.
- Pellets' share of the trap flux:
  - Kattegat: <5 % (1995) or ~17 % (1997, own calculation)
  - Gullmar: copepods dominated the faecal flux, which was about half or more of the zooplankton-mediated flux
  - Review: mostly <40 % (Turner 2015)

## 6. Implications / suggested parameterisation (synthesis, not from a single source)

- Replace "100 % exported" with a layer-resolved loss. For each layer (mixed layer M(t), subsurface layer to
  30 m), pellet mass leaves at s = v/H and is remineralised in place at r(T, season). Plausible defaults from the
  sources: v ~ 5-30 m d-1 for small-copepod pellets, larger for Calanus; r ~ 0.5 d-1 (winter) to 2.5 d-1 (spring
  bloom). The steady-state escape fraction is s/(s + r).
- Optionally route a fixed share (Olesen 2005: >50 %) of zooplankton egesta into a non-sinking, fast-remineralising
  pool (degrades ~0.5 d-1, sinks ~5 m d-1).
- A simpler fixed split consistent with the literature: retain ~60-80 % of zooplankton egesta above 30 m in
  summer, ~20-40 % in spring/winter. For a calanoid-dominated Skagerrak spring, Gullmar (16 % recycled at 30 m)
  argues for the low end.
- Retained egesta should regenerate N preferentially to C (Kattegat C/N 8.6 trap vs 6.4 suspended).
