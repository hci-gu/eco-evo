# Prey refuge against harbour seals: visibility_floor for seals -> gadoids and seals -> pelagic_fish

Scope: harbour seal (*Phoca vitulina*) in the Skagerrak/North Sea. Grey seal (*Halichoerus grypus*) and other-region evidence is marked **[FALLBACK]**. Model context: `visibility_floor` = fraction of resting ("hidden") prey biomass that a predator can still reach. Today the seal pairs have no override, so they inherit the prey defaults: gadoids 0.6 and pelagic_fish 0.35. For comparison, the existing pair overrides are porpoises -> herring 0.95, porpoises -> gadoids 0.75, gadoids -> herring 0.25 and herring -> zooplankton 0.5. Darkness is modelled separately, through the light-dependent attack rate.

Research note: about 25 tool calls. int-res.com (MEPS), ResearchGate, ScienceDirect and Semantic Scholar returned 403 or 429 errors, so several primary full texts (Bowen et al. 2002, the captive herring-pursuit study) were seen only as search snippets. Those items are marked as such.

## Q1. Buried/resting flatfish, bottom-sheltering gadoids and buried sandeel: how detectable and catchable are they for harbour seals?

### Takeaway
Hiding on or in the bottom is the state harbour seals are specialised to exploit. Their vibrissae locate the breathing currents of buried flatfish, they dig prey out of the sediment, and in 2020 the most numerous prey in Skagerrak harbour-seal scats was sandeel, a fish that spends the night buried in sand. No study gives a numerical "fraction of buried fish detected", but the evidence points to burying or resting on the bottom giving little protection against this predator.

### Cited Findings
- Niesterok et al. (2017a, J Exp Biol 220:174-185) tested three harbour seals on a platform of eight nozzles emitting artificial flatfish breathing currents. The seals found the active nozzle better than chance, with or without blindfolds, and moved their snout straight to the opening that emitted the stimulus. They could use the direction of an incoming current to locate its source "without additional search effort". — [JEB abstract](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/220/2/174/18651/Hydrodynamic-detection-and-localization-of); [JEB commentary "Super sensitive seal whiskers feel fish breath"](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/220/2/133/18656/Super-sensitive-seal-whiskers-feel-fish-breath)
- Niesterok, Dehnhardt & Hanke (2017b, J Exp Biol 220:2364) measured the detection threshold for a breathing current as maximum water velocity in the current: 4.2 cm/s for one seal and 3.7 cm/s for the other. This is the first vibrissal threshold measured on a moving harbour seal under near-natural conditions. — [JEB PDF](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/220/13/2364/1896126/jeb158055.pdf)
- Press coverage quotes Hanke as speculating that flatfish might stop breathing to avoid detection as a seal passes. This is speculation, not a tested result. — [divernet](https://divernet.com/scuba-diving/hold-your-breath-flatties-its-a-seal/); [Cosmos](https://cosmosmagazine.com/science/biology/super-sensitive-seal-whiskers-pinpoint-fish-breath)
- Harbour seals use "ambush, active search or digging with final pursuit" depending on prey type, and eat more benthic than pelagic species. — [NAMMCO harbour seal species page](https://nammco.no/harbour-seal/)
- Kappa, Härkönen, Freitas, Carroll & Harding (2026, Mar Mamm Sci 42(1):e70069; doi 10.1111/mms.70069) analysed 73 scats from three haul-outs in the northern (Norwegian) Skagerrak. Shares of otoliths in 2020 were sandeel 39.6 %, Norway pout 21.8 %, a grouped "herring/pollack/saithe" category 12.8 % and blue whiting 7.9 %. The gadid family made up 50.3 %. In 1977/78/1989 the shares were Norway pout 34.7 %, sandeel 11.7 % and herring 10.3 %. The diet held 19 fish species. These figures come from a secondary summary (Oceanbites); the paper itself was not retrieved. Flatfish are not mentioned in the summary text. — [Oceanbites summary](https://oceanbites.org/fishy-changes-to-seal-diet-over-time/)
- In a northern Skagerrak (Gothenburg) thesis, flatfish supplied 46 % of the energy in the recent harbour-seal diet. Herring's energy share fell from about 24 % to 2.8 %. — [GUPEA thesis record](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/cdff6716-c000-48f8-8a86-684cee6b2510)
- Sørlie et al. (2020), Norwegian Skagerrak (Telemark/Aust-Agder): haddock, pollack and saithe made up 32.7 % of the diet, Norway pout/poor cod/bib 12.5 %, plaice 12.4 %, herring 10.0 % and cod 0.7 %. By biomass, plaice was the largest contributor and cod 2.3 %. — [IMR Brage record](https://imr.brage.unit.no/imr-xmlui/handle/11250/2682796)
- Sandeel behaviour: lesser sandeel bury in the seabed at night and come out by day to school and feed on zooplankton. Buried fish sit typically 1-4 cm deep in anoxic sediment and pump water through the interstices to ventilate. They prefer sandy sediments. — [Scottish Government sandeel evidence review](https://gov.scot/publications/sandeel-consultation-review-scientific-evidence/pages/2); [ScienceDaily (DTU)](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/12/111206114219.htm)
- **[FALLBACK, grey seal]** Telemetry of Farne Islands grey seals: at-sea destinations were often localised gravel/sand areas, the preferred burrowing habitat of sandeel, an important grey-seal prey. Dives there were inferred to go to the seabed, i.e. foraging. Time of day is not reported. — [Marine Scotland summary](https://marine.gov.scot/sma/node/17251)
- **[FALLBACK, grey seal, North Sea]** Sandeels were a major prey of central North Sea grey seals. Between 1985 and 2002 the diet shifted toward haddock, plaice and other benthic prey. — [SMRU seal diet leaflet](https://synergy.st-andrews.ac.uk/smru/files/2016/08/Seal-Diet-Leaflet.pdf)
- **[FALLBACK, harbour seal, Sable Island, NW Atlantic]** Bowen et al. (2002, MEPS 244:235-245) put animal-borne video on 39 adult male harbour seals and recorded 1,094 capture attempts. The seals mainly targeted sand lance and flounders, occasionally salmonids and gadoids, and their tactics depended on prey type. Only the search snippet was available; capture-success values per prey type could not be extracted. — [MEPS PDF link (not retrievable, 429)](https://www.int-res.com/articles/meps2002/244/m244p235.pdf); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/250217790_Prey-dependent_foraging_tactics_and_prey_profitability_in_a_marine_mammal)
- Head-mounted accelerometers on a captive harbour seal, filmed with high-speed video, distinguish raptorial and suction feeding. Multi-sensor tags on wild Wadden Sea harbour seals (12 offshore trips, 8 seals) showed capture-attempt rates in transit similar to those offshore. — [Sci Rep 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-021-85376-2); [PMC7985366](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7985366/)

### Inferences
- In the model, a hidden gadoid or flatfish is one lying still on or in the bottom. For a vibrissa-guided, digging, benthic forager this is the normal prey state, not a refuge. A resting flatfish still ventilates and so leaves a detectable hydrodynamic trail; the threshold of a few cm/s is within the range of a fish's breathing current, as the JEB study was designed to show.
- Sandeel (about 40 % of otoliths in 2020 scats) and flatfish (46 % of diet energy) both rely on burial or crypsis as their main defence. Their dominance in the diet is strong indirect evidence that burial gives little protection against harbour seals. The model's gadoids group stands in for flatfish and partly for these benthic-cryptic prey, so this applies directly to seals -> gadoids. Note that sandeel itself has no model group.
- Holding breath (Hanke's speculation) would be the only flatfish counter-strategy against vibrissal detection, and it is unquantified.

### Gaps
- No field or lab study gives a fraction of buried or resting flatfish detected and caught by harbour seals. The Niesterok work shows the ability to localise, not a capture rate in the wild.
- Bowen et al. 2002 capture success per prey type (sand lance vs flounder) could not be extracted: the full text was blocked.
- No direct (video) evidence was found of seals digging buried sandeel out of the sediment at night in the North Sea or Skagerrak. The NAMMCO statement on "digging" is general.
- The Kappa et al. 2026 figures are from a secondary summary. The "herring/pollack/saithe" grouping looks odd (it may really be a pollack/saithe group) and should be checked in the paper.

## Q2. Cod/haddock/saithe/pollack sheltering in reefs, kelp or crevices vs seals

### Takeaway
No study was found that directly measures seal success on gadoids sheltering in reef, kelp or crevices. Indirectly, the structure-associated gadoids (haddock, pollack, saithe) are the single largest prey category of harbour seals in the Norwegian Skagerrak, so complex habitat does not stop seals from exploiting them.

### Cited Findings
- Haddock, pollack and saithe made up 32.7 % of harbour-seal diet in the Norwegian Skagerrak, the largest category. Cod was only 0.7 % (2.3 % by biomass). — [Sørlie et al. 2020, IMR Brage](https://imr.brage.unit.no/imr-xmlui/handle/11250/2682796)
- In 2020 the gadid family made up 50.3 % of otoliths in northern Skagerrak scats, and cod decreased relative to historical data. — [Oceanbites summary of Kappa et al. 2026](https://oceanbites.org/fishy-changes-to-seal-diet-over-time/)
- **[FALLBACK, North Sea EwE]** In the Mackinson & Daskalov North Sea model, adult saithe were eaten only by seals (in the diet data used), and adult cod only by seals, cod and monkfish. Seals are thus treated as a main predator of large demersal gadoids. — [Cefas Tech Rep 142, section 4.1](https://www.cefas.co.uk/publications/techrep/tech142.pdf)
- **[FALLBACK, other species]** One leopard seal individual used prey-specific stalk-and-flush techniques, showing that pinnipeds can have tactics aimed at prey hiding in structure. — [Biotelemetry 2015, leopard seals at Livingston Island](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40317-015-0059-2)
- **[FALLBACK, grey seal]** Grey seals foraging in shallow water ate more flatfish and cephalopods, while those over deeper sandy substrate ate more pelagic and bentho-pelagic prey (blue whiting, sandeel). The authors suggest that shallow water lets seals spend more time foraging along the benthos. — [PLoS ONE 2019, via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/8f8d4e3ae25947b6ab848a0ca9f9cbee)

### Inferences
- Many Skagerrak gadoids are hard-bottom and kelp associated (pollack, juvenile saithe, cod). Seals still take them in large numbers, so shelter in structure probably reduces exposure only somewhat. A floor in the range of the current gadoid default (0.6) or higher is consistent with this. Nothing found argues for a lower value.
- Of the gadoids, cod is the one most associated with crevices and refuges, and its low and falling diet share may partly reflect refuge use. It more plausibly reflects low cod abundance in coastal Skagerrak, though, and that belongs in biomass, not in the floor.

### Gaps
- No animal-borne camera or accelerometer study was found of harbour or grey seals hunting gadoids in kelp, reef or crevice habitat in the NE Atlantic.
- The seal-specific refuge value of kelp vs open bottom is unquantified.
- A search summary mentioned Weddell seals flushing small fishes from ice cracks with air bubbles; no citable source was retrieved.

## Q3. Herring and sprat: schooling, diel vertical migration and seal success

### Takeaway
Clupeid schooling and diel vertical migration are real anti-predator strategies. Against fish predators, kills concentrate at dusk and dawn when schools form or dissolve. Harbour seals actively pursue schools in captivity. In the wild they appear to take herring opportunistically, favouring juveniles and not tracking dense adult aggregations. In the Skagerrak, herring has fallen to a minor diet item (2.8 % of energy). Baltic grey seals eat mostly herring, so schooling does not stop pinnipeds when herring is the dominant available prey.

### Cited Findings
- Captive harbour seals pursuing schooling herring "actively pursued the fish, mostly changing from one side of the school to the other", and attacked from above or below depending on approach direction. The paper is "How harbor seals (*Phoca vitulina*) pursue schooling herring", Mammalian Biology 2015, seen as a snippet only. — [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1616504715000397)
- **[FALLBACK, Pacific harbour seal]** Thomas et al. (2011, MEPS 441:225-239): harbour-seal use of herring areas was highest outside the spawning season, and seal diving did not follow the vertical distribution of herring. In the spawn season, herring in the diet was 74 % juveniles and 26 % adults; post-spawn it was 37 % and 63 %. The authors attribute this to low energy density of spawning adults and profitable alternative prey. — [MEPS abstract](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v441/p225-239); [Thomas et al. 2011 PDF](https://mmru.ubc.ca/wp-content/pdfs/Thomas%20et%20al%202011.pdf)
- **[FALLBACK, Pacific harbour seal]** In the San Juan Islands, seal predation on schooling fish involved juvenile sandlance or herring, and surface attacks on forage-fish schools were more common than large-fish captures. Night and subsurface feeding were not assessed. — [Tethys record](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/publications/seal-predation-salmon-forage-fish-schools-function-tidal-currents-san-juan-islands)
- In the northern Skagerrak, herring's share of harbour-seal diet energy fell from about 24 % to 2.8 %. — [GUPEA thesis](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/cdff6716-c000-48f8-8a86-684cee6b2510). Kappa et al. 2026 likewise report a dramatic decrease in herring (historical 10.3 % of otoliths). — [Oceanbites](https://oceanbites.org/fishy-changes-to-seal-diet-over-time/)
- Herring made up 10.0 % of the diet in the Norwegian Skagerrak (Sørlie et al. 2020). — [IMR Brage](https://imr.brage.unit.no/imr-xmlui/handle/11250/2682796)
- **[FALLBACK, Baltic grey seal]** Lundström et al. (2007), 145 grey-seal digestive tracts from 2001-2004: herring was the dominant prey by both number and biomass whatever estimation method was used. Sprat came second in the main Baltic. — [NAMMCO Sci Publ 6, abstract](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/NAMMCOSP/article/view/2733)
- **[Baltic, fish predator]** Sprat and herring schools form at dawn and break up at dusk below a light threshold. At night the fish disperse randomly higher in the water column. — [Aquat Living Resour 2003](https://www.alr-journal.org/10.1016/S0990-7440(03)00007-X). In the Bornholm Basin, cod predation on sprat happened mainly at dusk and dawn, during school dissolution and formation. — [MEPS 583:195-209](https://int-res.com/abstracts/meps/v583/p195-209)
- **[Herring anti-predator behaviour, not pinniped]** A wild-caught school of about 60,000 herring in a sea cage showed threat-sensitive collective vertical avoidance, strongest when visual and water-displacement cues were combined. — [Rieucau et al. 2014, PLoS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3906054/).

### Inferences
- Unlike porpoises, seals hunt visually and with their vibrissae, not by echolocation. Schooling (confusion effect, collective evasion) should therefore protect herring more against seals than against porpoises (0.95), but less than against gadoids (0.25), since a seal is faster and more manoeuvrable than a cod-sized fish. Day/night is handled by the attack-rate light multiplier, so the floor should reflect only the refuge value of the school.
- Herring's low and falling share in Skagerrak seal diet is mainly a **selectivity/availability** signal: benthic foraging habit, herring abundance and distribution offshore, low energy density of spawning adults. It is not evidence that schooling makes herring uncatchable for seals. That signal belongs in the attack rate, diet preference or spatial overlap, **not** in `visibility_floor`. Lowering the floor to reproduce a small diet share would mix two separate mechanisms.
- The Baltic grey-seal evidence (herring the dominant prey) shows that pinnipeds catch schooling clupeids efficiently when these are the most available prey.

### Gaps
- No capture-success rate for harbour seals on herring or sprat schools (captive or wild) was obtained; the captive study was seen only as a snippet.
- No North Sea or Skagerrak study was found that documents seals targeting, or avoiding, deep daytime herring schools vs dispersed night-time herring.
- North Sea clupeid diel vertical migration data were not retrieved (Baltic only).
- A search summary described Nottestad et al. (2002, ICES J Mar Sci): Norwegian spring-spawning herring schools under fin-whale attack were large, dense and deep, which the authors read as risk-averse behaviour. No reliable source URL was obtained, so it is not used as a finding.

## Q4. How other models parameterise refuge and vulnerability for pinniped predation (EwE)

### Takeaway
The North Sea and Baltic EwE models give seals no prey-type-specific refuge. The North Sea model (Mackinson & Daskalov 2007) uses one vulnerability for seals across all fish prey: v = 2 (the default) in the fitted 1973 model and v = 3 in the fitted 1991 model. The Baltic model (Costalago et al. 2018) sets k = 100 for grey seals, justified explicitly by the statement that fish prey have "no refuge from predation". Neither model distinguishes flatfish or gadoids from herring for seals.

### Cited Findings
- **Mackinson & Daskalov (2007), North Sea EwE, Cefas Science Series Tech Rep 142** — [PDF](https://www.cefas.co.uk/publications/techrep/tech142.pdf); text extracted locally with pdftotext:
  - Default vulnerability is 2 (Fig. 4.1, "default vulnerability = 2"). With feeding-time factors and predator effects included, "at vulnerabilities above 3 the dynamics became unstable" (section 4.1).
  - In Table 4.5 (fitted 1991 model) the seal column (predator 3) holds **3** in every fish-prey row read: adult cod, whiting, haddock, saithe, large and small other gadoids, monkfish, adult herring, sprat, horse mackerel, sandeels, plaice, dab, long-rough dab, flounder, sole, lemon sole, witch, turbot/brill, megrim, dragonets and small demersal fish. In Table 4.6 (fitted 1973 model) the seal column holds **2** in every fish-prey row (cod, whiting, haddock, saithe, other gadoids, herring juvenile and adult, sprat, sandeels, plaice...). Column alignment was read from a text-layout extraction and mapped to the header positions. I am confident that the value is uniform across prey within each model, but the reading should be checked against the PDF.
  - Initial vulnerabilities were set by reasoning: predators far from carrying capacity get higher v, low-trophic-level and juvenile consumers lower v. They were then fitted by optimisation only for groups with good time series (mostly MSVPA species) or high sensitivity. "Vulnerabilities for other groups were left at the initial values" (section 4.2). It is therefore unclear whether the seal values were fitted or assumed.
  - Seal group info: feeding-time factor 2, feeding-time adjustment rate 0.1, predator effect on feeding time 0, QBmax/QBo 1000 (Ecosim group-info table in section 4.1).
  - The North Sea model includes both grey and harbour seals in one "Seals" group (section 15.3), with consumption Q/B 26.84 (grey) and about 30 (harbour seal, Trites formula).
- **Costalago et al. (2018, Ambio), Baltic Proper EwE, Electronic Supplementary Material, Appendix S2** — [ESM PDF](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1007%2Fs13280-018-1131-y/MediaObjects/13280_2018_1131_MOESM1_ESM.pdf); [article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs13280-018-1131-y):
  - "When vulnerability multipliers are high (kij >> 2), the predators can increase their consumption of prey when their biomass increases... 'top-down control'... typically occurs when the prey has no refuge from predation and/or the predator is far from carrying capacity... Both criteria are fulfilled in the case of grey seals and their fish prey. Thus, we specified a value of kij = 100 for this parameter for both juvenile and adult seals."
  - Maximum relative feeding time is 5 (juveniles) and 10 (adults), above the default 2, because "seals do not need to limit the time spent feeding to avoid predation". Feeding-time adjustment rate is 0.5, predator effect on feeding time 0, and QBmax/QBo 500 (100-1000 made no difference). Grey seals are described as opportunistic, feeding mainly on the most available prey.
- **[FALLBACK, Strait of Georgia]** Li, Ainsworth & Pitcher (2010) found that the effect of seal removal on herring depends strongly on the herring-to-hake vulnerability parameterisation. This shows that seal-related results in EwE are sensitive to v, but it gives no seal-specific refuge value. — [UBC cIRcle record](https://open.library.ubc.ca/cIRcle/collections/52383/items/1.0074795)

### Inferences
- EwE vulnerability (k = maximum / baseline predation mortality, the foraging-arena exchange) is not the same concept as `visibility_floor`. It lumps together spatial refuges, behaviour and predator saturation. The one clear qualitative signal is that neither model gives seals a lower vulnerability for flatfish or gadoids than for herring. Where a model makes a statement at all (Baltic), it is "no refuge". The North Sea values 2-3 are close to the generic default and partly constrained by model stability (v > 3 unstable), so they are weak evidence of a real refuge.
- A very rough heuristic mapping, my own derivation and not from the sources: under symmetric foraging-arena exchange, the fraction of prey in the vulnerable state at baseline is about (k-1)/(2k). That gives 0.25 at k = 2, 0.33 at k = 3 and about 0.5 at k = 100. This measures all biomass, including prey that are not hiding, so it cannot be transferred to the floor directly. It is noted here only to discourage reading k = 2-3 as "seals reach 25-33 % of hidden prey".

### Gaps
- No Kattegat/Skagerrak EwE model with harbour-seal vulnerabilities was found.
- The Baltic seal vulnerabilities of Tomczak et al. and other Baltic EwE models, and EcoBase entries, were not checked.
- The North Sea 1991/1973 column reading was not verified against the typeset PDF table.

## Q5. Synthesis: suggested visibility_floor for seals -> gadoids and seals -> pelagic_fish

### Takeaway
(My inference, based on the findings above.) Seals -> gadoids should get a pair override **above** the gadoid default: about **0.85**, range **0.75-0.95**. Seals -> pelagic_fish can keep or modestly raise the herring default: about **0.4**, range **0.3-0.5**. The two seal floors should therefore differ, in the opposite direction to how a "hiding" refuge works against fish predators: bottom hiding helps very little against seals, schooling helps somewhat.

### Cited Findings
- The evidence base for these values is the findings cited under Q1-Q4, especially Niesterok et al. 2017a/b ([JEB](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article-abstract/220/2/174/18651/Hydrodynamic-detection-and-localization-of)), the 2020 Skagerrak scat composition (sandeel 39.6 %, gadids 50.3 % of otoliths; [Oceanbites/Kappa et al. 2026](https://oceanbites.org/fishy-changes-to-seal-diet-over-time/)), flatfish 46 % of diet energy ([GUPEA](https://gupea.ub.gu.se/items/cdff6716-c000-48f8-8a86-684cee6b2510)), and Baltic EwE "no refuge", k = 100 ([Costalago et al. 2018 ESM](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1007%2Fs13280-018-1131-y/MediaObjects/13280_2018_1131_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)).

### Inferences
**seals -> gadoids (gadoids plus the flatfish stand-in): suggest 0.85 (range 0.75-0.95), i.e. raise above the inherited 0.6.**
- In the model, hidden gadoids and flatfish sit still on or in the bottom. That is the state harbour seals are equipped to find: vibrissae read breathing currents, even blindfolded, and seals dig or ambush on the bottom. Buried and benthic-cryptic prey (sandeel, flatfish, Norway pout, structure-associated gadoids) dominate Skagerrak seal diet.
- The floor should be at or above porpoises -> gadoids (0.75). Porpoises echolocate but are not benthic-dig specialists, and flatfish are acoustically cryptic, whereas seals are the specialist predator of resting benthic fish.
- The upper end, 0.95, treats bottom hiding as nearly worthless against seals, in line with the Baltic "no refuge" EwE assumption and with porpoise -> herring 0.95. The lower end, 0.75, leaves some refuge for gadoids in kelp or crevices and for flatfish that stop ventilating (unquantified). I found nothing in the literature that would justify keeping or lowering 0.6 for this pair.
- Caveat: the model's gadoids group also contains pelagic-schooling gadoids (saithe schools, Norway pout, whiting), whose rest-as-hide behaviour is less bottom-bound. If those dominate the group's biomass in the model, the lower end (about 0.75) is more defensible.

**seals -> pelagic_fish (herring, sprat): suggest 0.4 (range 0.3-0.5), i.e. inherit 0.35 or raise slightly.**
- Seals are visual and vibrissal pursuit predators without echolocation, so schooling gives real protection (confusion, collective evasion). The floor should be well below porpoise -> herring (0.95).
- Seals are faster and more manoeuvrable than gadoid predators and in captivity switch sides and attack schools from above and below. That argues for a value at or slightly above gadoids -> herring (0.25) and herring's own default (0.35).
- Night dispersal (a weaker refuge at night) is already handled by the light multiplier on the attack rate, so it should not raise the floor.
- Do **not** lower this floor to reproduce herring's small share (2.8 % of energy) in recent Skagerrak seal diet. That reflects prey selection, spatial overlap, herring abundance and energy density (Thomas et al. 2011: seals did not follow adult herring aggregations), which belong in the attack rate, diet preference or overlap. The Baltic grey-seal diet, dominated by herring, shows that pinnipeds catch schooling clupeids readily when these are the main available prey.

**Should the seal floors differ from the prey defaults?** Yes for gadoids: the default 0.6 is a compromise across gadoid predators, and seals are the predator for which bottom hiding is least effective. Hardly for pelagic_fish: 0.35-0.4 fits; an override to 0.4 is optional.

### Gaps
- None of the suggested values is backed by a measured fraction. They are calibrated by ordering against the existing pair overrides and by qualitative sensory and diet evidence. A sensitivity check with floors at the range endpoints in the model is advisable.
- Seal capture success on resting vs active benthic fish is not quantified anywhere I could find. That measurement, for example from animal-borne video with prey state annotated, would be needed to pin the gadoid floor down properly.
