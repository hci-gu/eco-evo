## Q1. Harbour seal vision in dim light: at what illuminance does it stop being useful for finding fish, and can we turn that into a sun elevation at 20-100 m?

### Takeaway
Harbour seal eyes are built for dim light: a rod-dominated retina, a very thick tapetum lucidum covering the whole fundus, and a large pupil. Dark adaptation is faster than in humans, and light sensitivity is higher. **No published absolute threshold in lux for harbour seals, and no "vision fails at X lux" value for detecting fish, was found.** The only quantified low-light operating point is functional, purely rod-based brightness discrimination at about 0.9 lx ambient (n = 2, lab). A sun elevation can therefore be computed only by borrowing a threshold (own calc below). The physics is clear about one thing: at 40-50 m in Skagerrak water there is still a large light difference between day and night, but at about 100 m it is close to dark even at noon.

### Cited Findings
- **Dark adaptation and relative sensitivity (lab, behavioural psychophysics, one animal per species: harbour seal, California sea lion, northern elephant seal, plus one human).** "In comparison to the human subject, both the California sea lion and the harbor seal dark-adapted relatively quickly and were more light sensitive... suited for vision in the moderately dim shallow-water environments in which they dive to forage." The elephant seal reached full dark adaptation in less than half the time and was "significantly more light sensitive". The abstract gives no threshold values. - [Levenson & Schusterman 1999, Mar Mamm Sci (abstract via Crossref)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-7692.1999.tb00892.x)
- The 1997 university press piece on the same experiments says only one animal per species was tested. The deep diver's pupil stayed constricted at a light level "about 100,000 times dimmer" than the level that fully dilated the other two animals' pupils, and it detected fainter flashes. No harbour-seal-specific numbers are given. - [UCSC Currents 1997](https://www1.ucsc.edu/oncampus/currents/97-08-18/seal.sight.htm)
- **[proxy: elephant seal]** Full dark adaptation in about 6 min, close to the 6.8 min it takes to reach foraging depth. "The eyes of harbor seals take... three times as long as that of the elephant seal to dark adapt" (still much faster than terrestrial mammals). Secondary source citing Levenson & Schusterman 1999. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022, "Phocid Sensory Systems and Cognition", in Ethology and Behavioral Ecology of Phocids, Springer (PDF), section 2.2.1](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- **[proxy: elephant seal]** "An elephant seal can become completely dark-adapted in the time it takes (~5 min) to dive from the bright surface to foraging depths of 500 m." - [Schusterman et al. 1999, JASA 105 (conference abstract)](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.425672)
- Pupil area in darkness: harbour seal 162 mm2, against 422 mm2 in elephant seals, 124 mm2 in ringed seals and 208 mm2 in harp seals. The ratio of maximum to minimum pupil area is 71 in harbour seals and 469 in elephant seals. Secondary, citing Levenson & Schusterman 1997. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- Seals have "the thickest tapeta among vertebrates" (up to 35 cell layers; 50 in elephant seals), and it lines the whole fundus. Fewer than 2% of photoreceptors are cones, the retina is "dominated by rod photoreceptors", and seals are "highly sensitive to small amounts of light". Secondary review (anatomy). - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- **Operating point at about 1 lx (lab, n = 2 harbour seals).** The test chamber's ambient light was 0.9 lx (0.5 cd/m2) and stimuli were 0.05-13 cd/m2. The seals discriminated brightness and showed "strong evidence for purely scotopic (rod-based) vision at light levels that lead to mesopic (rod-cone-based) vision in other mammals". Brightness discrimination threshold: Weber contrast 14%. Rod lambda-max is 496 nm. - [Scholtyssek, Kelber & Dehnhardt 2015, Anim Cogn 18:551](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4320766)
- **Acuity vs luminance (in air, lab, n = 3 harbour seals).** In bright light, aerial acuity matches acuity in clear water. "When the pupil dilates with decreasing luminance, visual acuity decreases". The pupillometry series covered 80 down to 0.15 cd/m2. The abstract gives no acuity values at each luminance. - [Hanke & Dehnhardt 2009, J Comp Physiol A 195:643](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00359-009-0439-2); [ResearchGate figure listing luminance levels](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Results-of-pupillometry-One-example-frame-obtained-with-the-camera-monitoring-the-left_fig3_24271888)
- **Turbidity, not light, limits acuity under water (lab, n = 2 male harbour seals, 2 m viewing distance).** Clear-water acuity was 5.5' and 12.7' (arc minutes) for the two seals. It worsened by 7.4' and 6.0' per FNU, "a dramatic loss of visual acuity even at moderate levels of turbidity". Wadden Sea foraging sites exceeded 40 FNU. - [Weiffen et al. 2006, Vision Res 46](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2005.08.015)
- "Whereas visual acuity in air is strongly affected by available light, visual acuity in water decreases with turbidity." Under good light in clear water a seal "can visually track and hunt its prey" (citing Kilian et al. 2015), and "vision might especially come into play in close vicinity to prey". - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- Harbour seals perceive many stars in the night sky and can tell a single star from a starry sky (Mauck et al. 2005, 2008), which shows that they see at night in air. Secondary. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- Surface illuminance vs sun elevation, the curve already used in this project: about 500 lx at 0 deg, about 3.4 lx at -6 deg, then x0.1 per 2 deg (Koomen et al. 1952) down to a starlight floor of about 0.001 lx (moon up to about 0.2 lx). This comes from the project's earlier gadoid notes, which cite [Koomen et al. 1952, JOSA 42:353](https://opg.optica.org/josa/abstract.cfm?uri=josa-42-5-353) and [Spitschan et al. 2016, Sci Rep 6:26756](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep26756). Above the horizon the notes use Haurwitz clear-sky radiation x 115 lx per W/m2 (about 5,200 lx at +5 deg and about 11,400 lx at winter noon, +8.4 deg at 58 N).
- Local foraging depth: the Mareld EIA says harbour and grey seals forage "down to about 40-50 m" in shallow coastal areas (citing SLU Artdatabanken 2021). Recorded in the project's notes: `research_notes/Parametervärden för sälar i Skagerrak/distribution_and_impacts.md`, citing `MKB Mareld.pdf` p. 133-134.

### Inferences
- **Own calc: light at depth.** The attenuation factor exp(-Kd z), ignoring surface reflection, is:

  | depth | Kd 0.15 /m | Kd 0.20 /m |
  |---|---|---|
  | 20 m | 5.0e-2 | 1.8e-2 |
  | 40 m | 2.5e-3 | 3.4e-4 |
  | 50 m | 5.5e-4 | 4.5e-5 |
  | 100 m | 3.1e-7 | 2.1e-9 |

  At 50 m (Kd 0.2) the light is about 0.5 lx at winter noon, about 4.5 lx at summer noon (100,000 lx at the surface) and about 5e-8 lx on a moonless night. **At the seals' 40-50 m foraging depth, day and night therefore differ by 7-8 decades, and daytime light is close to the 0.9 lx where harbour seal vision is proven to work.** At 100 m (Kd 0.2), summer noon gives only about 2e-4 lx, so a gadoid-hunting seal there would be in near-darkness all the time and a sun-elevation gate would do almost nothing.
- **Own calc: the sun elevation at which a borrowed threshold is reached at depth.** Surface lux needed = threshold / attenuation, mapped to elevation with the project's surface curve:

  | threshold at depth | depth | Kd 0.15 | Kd 0.20 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 0.01 lx (cod-pair value) | 20 m | 0.2 lx, about -8.5 deg | 0.55 lx, about -7.6 deg |
  | | 40 m | 4 lx, about -5.9 deg | 30 lx, about -4.1 deg |
  | | 50 m | 18 lx, about -4.6 deg | 220 lx, about -1.6 deg |
  | | 100 m | 3.2e4 lx, about +18 deg | not reached |
  | 0.001 lx (herring-pair, starlight-level rod vision) | 20 m | about -10.5 deg | about -9.6 deg |
  | | 40 m | about -7.9 deg | about -6.1 deg |
  | | 50 m | about -6.6 deg | about -4.4 deg |
  | | 100 m | about +4 deg | not reached |

- **Inference (ordering argument, not a derivation).** Harbour seals have a rod-dominated retina, a whole-fundus tapetum, are more sensitive than humans, and use pure rod vision at about 1 lx. Their dim-light vision should therefore be at least as good as cod's. If the cod threshold of 0.01-0.03 lx applies, the seal threshold at 40-50 m maps to about -6 to -2 deg. If the seal reaches the 0.001 lx herring level, it maps to about -8 to -4 deg. **A central value of theta ≈ -6 deg (plausible range -9 to -3) follows if rho < 1 is used.** This value comes from the cod/herring thresholds and the seals' 40-50 m foraging depth, not from a seal measurement.
- Inference: Weiffen et al. (2006) is about turbidity (Wadden Sea, >40 FNU), not darkness. Offshore Skagerrak water with Kd 0.15-0.2 is much clearer, so that paper should not be read as "seals cannot see where they forage" at the Mareld site.

### Gaps
- No absolute scotopic threshold (lux or cd/m2) for harbour seals was retrievable. Levenson & Schusterman 1999 contains threshold curves, but only the abstract was accessible, and it gives no values. A harbour seal's sensitivity relative to humans or cats in log units was not found.
- No study measured underwater fish-detection distance or capture success against light level for any phocid in the lab. Hanke & Dehnhardt 2009 measured grating acuity in air, and only down to 0.15 cd/m2.
- The full text of Kilian et al. 2015 (visual prey tracking in harbour seals) was not found or read.

## Q2. Vibrissae: hydrodynamic trail following, flatfish breathing currents, blindfolded prey capture, and blind wild seals in good condition

### Takeaway
Blindfolded harbour seals detect and follow fish-like hydrodynamic trails up to about 30-35 s old. They locate artificial flatfish breathing currents of about 4 cm/s. In the wild, apparently blind seals have been reported to thrive: harbour seals (Newby et al. 1970) and Saimaa ringed seals ("blind seals are managing well"). The one field study that directly compares the senses (elephant seals, aphotic depth) found that whiskers carry most of the prey detection and that vision contributes at most about 20% of feeding events. Almost all lab numbers come from 1-3 trained animals.

### Cited Findings
- **Trail following (lab, blindfolded harbour seals, n not stated in the abstract).** Blindfolded seals "detect and accurately follow hydrodynamic trails generated by a miniature submarine". "Water movements in the wake of fishes persist for several minutes", which "shows that hydrodynamic information can be used for long-distance prey location." - [Dehnhardt et al. 2001, Science 293:102](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1060514)
- Dipole detection threshold (lab): water velocity 245 um/s at 50 Hz, sensitive range 10-100 Hz (Dehnhardt et al. 1998a). Harbour seals "can detect and actively follow these hydrodynamic trails immediately after generation and up to 30 s later" (Dehnhardt 2001; Kilian 2010; Schulte-Pelkum 2007; Wieskotten 2010b). Secondary review. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF), section 2.3.4.2](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- **Biogenic trails (lab, 1 trail-following seal, 1 seal generating the trail).** The tracking seal "was able to detect and accurately track the hydrodynamic trail" left by another seal after it had left the water. It searched either along the trail or by crossing it in an undulating pattern, which "might allow a seal to relocate a lost trail or successfully track a fleeing, zigzagging prey fish". - [Schulte-Pelkum et al. 2007, J Exp Biol 210:781](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.02708)
- **Trail age (lab, 1 blindfolded harbour seal).** The seal recognised the direction of a fin-like paddle "when the hydrodynamic trail was up to 35 s old". - [Wieskotten et al. 2010a, J Exp Biol 213](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.041699)
- **Burst-and-glide (lab, 1 harbour seal).** Glide phases "had a negative impact on the trackability when trails were 15 s old". The seal lost trails more often where the trail switched from burst to glide. - [Wieskotten et al. 2010b, J Exp Biol 213:3734](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.047134)
- **Flatfish breathing currents, detection (lab, n = 3 harbour seals; half the trials blindfolded).** "In blindfolded and non-blindfolded trials, all seals performed significantly better than chance", from any approach direction. The seals moved their snout straight to the active opening, so they also extracted direction. - [Niesterok et al. 2017b, J Exp Biol 220](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.148676)
- **Flatfish breathing currents, threshold (lab, n = 2 harbour seals, blindfolded test trials).** Threshold peak water velocity was 4.2 cm/s and 3.7 cm/s. Background flow was 1.8-3.4 cm/s and swimming speed about 0.5 m/s. Swimming speed differed between successful and unsuccessful trials, so near threshold the seal must swim slowly. - [Niesterok, Dehnhardt & Hanke 2017a, J Exp Biol 220:2364](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.158055)
- Seals can read the size and movement direction of the trail's source, and can do so even from single vortices. This matters for fleeing fish, whose C-starts generate single vortex rings (Niesterok & Hanke 2013). Secondary. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF)](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- **[proxy: Saimaa ringed seal, field observation]** These seals "live in darkness under the ice cover for several months", and "it is known that blind seals are managing well in the lake". Underwater visibility in parts of their range is only 2 m. A vibrissa has more than 10 times the usual mammalian innervation. - [Hyvärinen 1989, J Zool (abstract via Crossref)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1989.tb05008.x)
- Wild harbour seals: "the occasional observation of seemingly blind seals apparently thriving in wild conditions (see, for example, Newby et al. 1970; Hyvärinen 1989)". The original is Newby, Hart & Arnold 1970, "Weight and blindness of harbor seals", J Mammal 51:152; it was not accessible, and n and the condition measures are unknown. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF), section 2.2](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- Counterpoint (anecdotal, rehabilitation, not wild adults): a stranded harbour seal pup that was functionally blind from bilateral cataracts was about 30% underweight and "could not catch fish". Another cataract-blind pup "could not learn to hunt". - [SEA LIFE / SeaWorld UK news](https://seaworldparks.co.uk/latest-news/now-you-seal-me-now-you-dont)
- **[proxy: northern elephant seal, field, aphotic depth; 5 seals with cheek video (9.4 h; 3 day and 2 night recordings), plus mandible accelerometers with light sensors]** Seals protracted their whiskers and whisked rhythmically to "detect, pursue, and capture prey". "A maximum of only 20% of overall feeding signals cooccurred with bioluminescence". Only 13.6 ± 16.3% of prey images included bioluminescence. Whisker protraction time did not differ with or without bioluminescence (P = 0.95). Seals "can chase and capture fleeing nonbioluminescent prey even after they are no longer visible to the camera". The authors' conclusion: "visual prey detection complemented and enhanced prey capture". - [Adachi et al. 2022, PNAS 119:e2119502119](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2119502119)
- [proxy: southern elephant seal] Only 1.7-26% of feeding events were associated with bioluminescence (ref. 27 in) - [Adachi et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2119502119)

### Inferences
- Inference: the vibrissal system works at long range (trails 15-35 s old) and for prey that is not moving (breathing currents). Cod, the other predator in the model, has no comparable sense, which is why the cod -> herring pair has a strong light effect (rho 0.6). Seals should therefore fall at or above the cod pair's rho.
- Own calc: a herring swimming at about 1 m/s leaves a trail 15-35 m long within the 15-35 s detection window. That puts the vibrissal search range at tens of metres, comparable to or larger than visual range at night or in turbid water. It is limited by glide phases and by the seal having to swim slowly (about 0.5 m/s near threshold).
- Inference: the blind-pup anecdotes describe naive juveniles that never learned to hunt. They do not test what an experienced adult can catch without vision. The wild reports (Newby 1970; Hyvärinen 1989) are the more relevant evidence, though they are qualitative.
- Inference: the elephant seal result bounds vision's share from above only in near-total darkness. Even there, whiskers did most of the work. It is the best field analogue for "how much does capture drop without light", and it points to a modest drop, but it is a different species, with different prey (myctophids, squid), at 200-1000 m.

### Gaps
- No experiment compared capture success or rate with and without blindfolds against live, free-swimming fish. All harbour seal tests used artificial trails, nozzles or trained conspecifics.
- The number of seals in Dehnhardt et al. 2001 and the trail lengths in metres were not in the abstract.
- The Newby et al. 1970 data (how many blind seals, what body weight) could not be read.
- How far trails from schooling herring (many overlapping wakes) or from bottom gadoids reach under natural background turbulence is unknown.

## Q3. Passive listening for prey sounds

### Takeaway
Passive listening is accepted as part of the phocid toolkit, and seals learn to home in on fish that carry acoustic tags. However, nothing in the sources quantifies it as a prey-detection channel against unmanipulated herring or gadoids. It supports "non-visual detection exists" but adds no number.

### Cited Findings
- Pinnipeds lack echolocation. "Instead, it appears that pinnipeds have evolved enhanced visual, tactile, and passive listening skills" that "allow pinnipeds to effectively forage... under water despite the lack of active biosonar". - [Schusterman et al. 2000, JASA 107](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.428506)
- "Some pinnipeds rely heavily on their vibrissae to forage benthically, while others are specialized in terms of visual sensitivity." - [Schusterman et al. 1999, JASA 105](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.425672)
- Seals "may use their hearing during foraging, as the sounds emitted by many fish species... could be used". Seals can preferentially target fish carrying acoustic transmitters (Cunningham et al. 2014b; Stansbury et al. 2015). Secondary. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022 (PDF), section 2.4.5](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- The Saimaa ringed seal's underwater clicks are of low frequency and intensity, and its adaptations for underwater sound localisation are "not well developed". The authors suggested echolocation, which later work rejects (see Schusterman 2000). - [Hyvärinen 1989](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1989.tb05008.x)

### Inferences
- Inference: gadoids (cod, haddock) are sound-producing fish, and herring produce some sounds. Listening could therefore help seals find gadoid aggregations in the dark. Without data it should be treated as a qualitative reason not to set rho low, not as a parameter input.

### Gaps
- No study found on seals locating wild, untagged herring or gadoids by their sounds, and no detection range.
- Sources for sound production by herring and gadoids were not gathered in this task (outside scope).

## Q4. Direct measurements of capture success or encounter rate in light vs dark

### Takeaway
**No harbour seal or grey seal study was found that reports prey-capture rate by day vs night or against light level.** The accelerometer method exists and has been validated on a captive harbour seal, but the published wild harbour seal dataset (Wadden Sea) averaged over the diel cycle instead of splitting it. The only light-resolved field data come from elephant seals (Q2).

### Cited Findings
- Head-mounted high-rate accelerometry gives reliable prey-capture cues: jerk peaks above 1000 m/s3 for both raptorial and suction feeding. Lab study, 1 trained harbour seal, validated with high-speed video. - [Ydesen et al. 2014, J Exp Biol 217](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.100016)
- Wild harbour seals (German Wadden Sea; 8 seals, 12 offshore trips; acceleration transients used as prey-capture attempts, PCA): foraging rates in transit were comparable to offshore rates. The outbound and inbound phases of about 24 h "were chosen primarily from the need to average over diel changes in prey and predator behaviour". No day/night PCA split is reported. The 1 Hz data and PCA timings are public. - [Vance et al. 2021, Sci Rep 11](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-85376-2); [dataset on figshare](https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Harbour_seal_offshore_foraging_data/14171315)
- North Sea DTAGs (3 harbour and 2 grey seals, up to 21 days; plus camera tags on 2 harbour seals): reports time budgets (5.3-12.4% of at-sea time resting on the bottom), but the abstract has no diel foraging split. - [Mikkelsen et al. 2019, Ecol Evol 9](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.4923)
- **[proxy: grey seal, Baltic, 13 rehabilitated juveniles, Argos, preprint]** Clear diel differences: "daytime dives were generally deeper and shorter, whereas nocturnal dives tended to be shallower and longer". Elevated nocturnal dive residuals "may reflect either favourable foraging opportunities or increased effort associated with dispersed prey". These are dive-shape data, not captures. - [Kuncienė et al. 2026, Research Square preprint](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9142555/v1)
- The project's earlier seal notes found no Skagerrak-Kattegat dive-depth or day/night foraging data (`research_notes/Parametervärden för sälar i Skagerrak/diet_and_foraging.md`, section 4).

### Inferences
- Inference: the Vance et al. 2021 public dataset (PCA timings plus GPS) would allow a direct day vs night PCA-rate comparison for wild harbour seals. That is the cheapest route to an empirical rho, if the project ever wants one.
- Inference: shallower nocturnal dives in grey seals are consistent with following prey that migrates upward at night, such as herring and sprat. That does not suggest the seals forage less in the dark.

### Gaps
- No diel prey-capture rate exists for any Phoca or Halichoerus population in the sources found. The rho values below are therefore inference, not measurement.

## Q5. Synthesis: is rho = 1 defensible for seals -> gadoids and seals -> pelagic_fish, or is 0.6-0.9 better supported? What theta if rho < 1?

### Takeaway
**All values here are inference; no direct measurement exists (Q4).** On balance the evidence supports **rho = 1 (no light dependence) as the default for both pairs**. The case is stronger for seals -> gadoids, since gadoids are bottom-associated, benthic prey can be found by vibrissae (breathing currents), and blind seals have been reported to thrive. For seals -> pelagic_fish, a mild light effect is a defensible alternative: **rho ≈ 0.8-0.9 with theta ≈ -6 deg (range -9 to -3)**. Seals should not get a cod-like 0.6.

### Cited Findings (what the inference rests on, all cited above)
- Anatomy and psychophysics: rod-dominated retina, a whole-fundus tapetum, more light-sensitive than humans, pure rod vision at about 0.9 lx. - [Levenson & Schusterman 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-7692.1999.tb00892.x); [Scholtyssek et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4320766)
- Non-visual long-range detection: blindfolded trail following with trails up to 30-35 s old, and breathing-current detection at about 4 cm/s. Lab, n = 1-3 animals per study. - [Dehnhardt et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1060514); [Wieskotten et al. 2010a](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.041699); [Niesterok et al. 2017a](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.158055)
- Blind wild seals reported to thrive: harbour seals (Newby 1970, via review) and Saimaa ringed seals. - [Hyvärinen 1989](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1989.tb05008.x); [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)
- [proxy: elephant seal, field] Vision contributes at most about 20% of feeding in darkness; whiskers are primary; vision "complemented and enhanced" capture. - [Adachi et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2119502119)
- The review's own framing: vision likely matters "especially... in close vicinity to prey" and for tracking prey in good light and clear water. - [Hanke & Reichmuth 2022](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.ucsc.edu/dist/d/804/files/2022/07/Hanke-Reichmuth2022_Chapter_PhocidSensorySystemsAndCogniti.pdf)

### Inferences
- **seals -> gadoids: rho = 1 (leave `dark_ratio` unset). Confidence: medium.** The reasons:
  1. Gadoids are near the bottom, and the breathing-current work shows vibrissae can find stationary or cryptic benthic fish.
  2. In the eastern grid where seals live (40-50 m), the vibrissal channel works regardless of light.
  3. (Conditional, not a reason at this site.) Below about 80-100 m the physics alone would make a sun-elevation gate nearly meaningless (Q1 table). But the EIA puts seal foraging at 40-50 m, where the gate does separate day from night by 7-8 decades of light. So rho = 1 for this pair rests on points 1, 2 and 4, not on depth.
  4. Blind wild seals reported in good condition.
  5. Model asymmetry: rho = 1 is the "no effect" setting, so we never claim more light dependence than the data show.

  A sensitivity value of 0.9 would be harmless.
- **seals -> pelagic_fish: default rho = 1; alternative rho = 0.8 (range 0.7-0.9), theta = -6 deg (range -9 to -3). Confidence: low.**
  - The case for some light effect: herring and sprat are fast, schooling, mid-water prey. Final pursuit of evasive prey benefits from vision, and Wieskotten 2010b shows glide phases degrade trail tracking. The elephant seal result says vision "enhanced" capture and was tied to up to 20% of feeding. Caveat on provenance: that bound says that in near-total darkness, elephant seals keep at least about 80% of captures with no light at all. It compares aphotic-plus-occasional-bioluminescence with fully dark, not daylight with dark; in daylight the visual share could be much larger than 20%. The day-to-night rho for seals on herring therefore cannot be derived from it, and 0.8-0.9 is a judgment, not a derived value.
  - The case against: herring rise toward the surface at night, which brings them into the seals' depth range and silhouettes them against the surface. Diel dive data from grey seals show shallower nocturnal dives, consistent with following prey upward rather than foraging less.
  - Ordering: rho(seal -> herring) should be above rho(cod -> herring) = 0.6. Seals have the cod's dim-light vision or better, plus a far-field vibrissal sense.
- **theta if rho < 1:** about -6 deg. This is not derived from a seal threshold, which does not exist in the sources. It comes from applying the cod (0.01-0.03 lx) and herring (0.001 lx) half-detection thresholds at 40-50 m (Kd 0.15-0.2), which gives -8 to -2 deg (Q1 table), and from the ordering argument that seal vision is at least as sensitive as cod vision. With the model's fixed 2-deg logistic width, -6 puts the transition in late civil to early nautical twilight. That is reasonable for a population of seals spread over 20-50 m.
- The confidence ranking is gadoids rho = 1 (medium) > pelagic rho = 1 (low-medium) ≈ pelagic rho 0.8 at -6 deg (low). Neither pair is supported at rho ≤ 0.6.
- Turbidity (Weiffen 2006) is a separate, stronger limit on seal vision than darkness in coastal and estuarine water. The model has no turbidity term, which is another reason not to put the vision-loss effect into the light gate.

### Gaps
- No direct day/night capture data for any harbour or grey seal population. A diel PCA analysis of the Vance et al. 2021 dataset would close this.
- No harbour seal absolute light threshold in lux, so theta cannot be derived from seal physiology.
- No data on how well seals hunt herring schools at night (whether vibrissae can track individual fish within a school's wake field).
