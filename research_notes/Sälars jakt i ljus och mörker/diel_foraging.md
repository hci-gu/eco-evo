# Diel and seasonal timing of foraging in free-ranging harbour seals (grey seals as fallback)

Scope: field evidence on whether harbour seals (Phoca vitulina) forage more, less or equally at night, how dive depth and prey type shift between day and night, and whether darkness limits foraging success. The aim is to set or reject a light-dependent seal attack rate (`dark_ratio`, `light_threshold_deg`) in a Skagerrak ecosystem model (57.8-58.3 N).

Source-access note: several key papers could only be read as abstracts or search snippets. Each finding below says whether it rests on full text, an abstract, a secondary citation or a search snippet. Region is given in [brackets] on every finding.

---

## 1. Telemetry/dive studies: foraging dives, dive depth and prey-capture attempts by time of day

### Takeaway
No harbour seal study I found shows lower foraging activity or lower capture success in darkness. Where a diel signal exists it points the other way: more dives, or deeper and more pelagic dives, at night. The authors attribute it to vertically migrating prey, not to light-limited hunting. In benthic-feeding populations (Moray Firth, Norwegian coast, Wadden Sea) diel variation in dive depth or type is reported as small or absent. One northern Norway study reports mainly daylight foraging, so the pattern depends on site and prey.

### Cited Findings
- [Svalbard, Arctic; full text] Blanchet, Lydersen, Ims & Kovacs 2015 (PLOS ONE 10:e0132686) tagged 30 harbour seals with CTD-SRDLs and recorded 189,104 dives. In autumn, "dives that occurred during the night (0-6 h and 18-24 h) were deeper, slightly longer and more numerous" than daytime dives (6-18 h). Diel period had "the greatest impact on the number of dives performed", and "most of the diving in this period of the year occurred during the night". Autumn dives were mostly < 50 m and ~3 min long — [Blanchet et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0132686)
- [Svalbard; full text] The authors link the night diving to likely autumn prey: Atlantic cod and shorthorn sculpin. Cod and haddock move up the water column at night. The paper reports no direct prey measurements — [Blanchet et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0132686)
- [Salish Sea, NE Pacific; full text] Wilson et al. 2014 found that "haul-out site, season, sex, and light (day/night) all influenced dive behavior". "During the day, at both sites during both seasons, Type I [deep benthic foraging] bouts were the predominant bout type used." "A greater percentage of Type II [shallow] bouts were performed at night during the breeding season." This shift is linked to salmon, which sit deeper by day and shallower at night. Day/night was set from monthly mean sunrise and sunset. Dive depth was not analysed by diel period — [Wilson et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3981695/)
- [Secondary citation in Wilson et al. 2014; Norway and Moray Firth] Bjørge et al. (1995) and Tollit et al. (1998) are cited as finding that "harbor seals fed mostly on benthic prey with little diurnal variation in dive depths or types". The Discussion adds that where "seals typically forage in the benthos ... no diurnal pattern in dive behavior is observed" — [Wilson et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3981695/)
- [Moray Firth, NE Scotland; abstract only] Tollit et al. 1998 (J. Zool. 244:209-222) studied five seals with time-depth recorders. They "were generally diving on, or close to, the sea-bed", mostly in 10-50 m over sandy sediments, with "occasional pelagic dives". The abstract does not mention diel variation, so the "little diurnal variation" statement rests on Wilson et al.'s reading of the full text — [Tollit et al. 1998, abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-zoology/article/abs/variations-in-harbour-seal-phoca-vitulina-diet-and-divedepths-in-relation-to-foraging-habitat/8F1A63244922970BCCBE678B7F5B7D9A)
- [Central Norway, Froan; snippet] Bjørge et al. 1995 tracked 13 seals, which were "typically foraging at or close to the sea bed". Foraging sites ranged from shallow kelp areas to 150-200 m muddy basins. Foraging dives were U-shaped and transit dives V-shaped, with a mean dive duration of 3.3 min — [Bjørge et al. 1995 (St Andrews portal)](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/habitat-use-and-diving-behaviour-of-harbour-seals-in-a-coastal-ar/); [IMR record](https://imr.brage.unit.no/imr-xmlui/handle/11250/105461?show=full)
- [Wadden Sea, NL/DE; database record] Ries et al. 1997 (Can. J. Zool. 75:2063-2068) used VHF to study 25 seals in October-November. The Marine Wildlife Behavior Database marks the diurnal pattern as "tested: No", so this study gives no diel result — [MWBD record](https://seamap.env.duke.edu/mwbd/attributes/Ries1997a)
- [German Wadden Sea; full text] Vance et al. 2021 (Sci. Rep.) used DTAG-4 accelerometer/magnetometer tags. Ten seals were tagged and eight of them made 12 offshore trips, in September 2016 and April/October 2017. Prey-capture attempts during travel matched the rates at the offshore sites. Seals rested a median 205.8 min/day, and resting rose by 36.9 min per day of the trip. The paper does not analyse prey-capture attempts by time of day; travel phases were set to 24 h to "average over diel changes in prey and predator behaviour" — [Vance et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7985366/); [press summary](https://aias.au.dk/events/show/artikel/harbour-seals-in-the-north-sea-eat-on-the-go)
- [German Wadden Sea; dataset] The open Vance et al. dataset includes the timing of prey-capture attempts on the offshore trips, so a day/night split could be computed from it — [Figshare dataset](https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Harbour_seal_offshore_foraging_data/14171315)
- [Northern Norway, Porsangerfjord; snippet of thesis abstract] Ramasco (PhD 2015, UiT) followed 15 seals in 2009-2013. Activity patterns "suggested that harbour seals mainly forage during daylight in autumn and spring in this area". The study also describes resting dives at sea. Small codfish were preferred in autumn; when they became less available, overwintering in cold deep water, seals switched to small pelagic fish — [Ramasco 2015, UiT Munin](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/8149)
- [Northern Norway, Vesterålen; snippet] Ramasco MSc 2008 tracked five juvenile females from August to March. They foraged close to the coast and shallow, with 50 % of dives at 12-32 m — [Ramasco 2008](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/1433)
- [Monterey Bay, California; search snippet] Mean dive counts were higher at night (574) than by day (337). Dive duration and depth did not differ between day and night — [ATOC study record](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftdtic:ADA330718)
- [Sable Island, NW Atlantic, breeding males; snippet] Deep dives were more frequent in daylight. Shallow dives predominated at twilight and night, which the authors link to mating behaviour rather than to foraging — [Marine Wildlife Behavior Database, Lesage 1999](https://seamap.env.duke.edu/mwbd/attributes/Lesage1999a)
- [Alaska and Canada; secondary citation] Studies there report that "foraging occurred most often at dusk", with a "distinct diurnal foraging pattern" tied to vertically migrating prey (Hastings et al.; Lesage et al.; Bowen et al.) — [cited in Wilson et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3981695/)
- [Mechanism; snippet] Dehnhardt, Mauch, Hanke & Bleckmann 2001 (Science 293:102-104) showed that harbour seals detect and follow hydrodynamic trails with their mystacial vibrissae. In follow-ups (e.g. J. Exp. Biol. 210:781, 2007) seals tracked a trail left by a miniature submarine or a conspecific. A goldfish wake can persist > 3 min. I did not see the original text confirming that the animals were blindfolded — [JEB 2007 follow-up](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/210/5/781/17300/Tracking-of-biogenic-hydrodynamic-trails-in); [ScienceDaily 2010](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/06/100610191044.htm)

### Inferences
- The "dive deeper by day, following vertically migrating prey" hypothesis is not supported for harbour seals in the sources I found. The only quantitative diel depth result (Svalbard, autumn) found deeper dives at night. Salish Sea seals made shallower bouts at night, and Monterey seals showed no depth difference. The direction depends on what the prey does.
- For benthic-feeding North Sea, Moray Firth and Norwegian seals, which match the model's `gadoids`, the best available statement is "little diurnal variation in dive depth or type". That points to roughly equal foraging by day and night, not to a darkness penalty.
- At the 20-100 m bottom depths of the Skagerrak model area, ambient light is far below surface levels even at noon. Seals also have a non-visual detection channel (vibrissae). Both make it implausible that surface sun elevation limits capture success on demersal fish. This is my inference; no study measured capture success against light.

### Gaps
- I found no study reporting prey-capture attempts (head jerks, jaw accelerometry) by day versus night for harbour seals in the North Sea, Wadden Sea, Kattegat or Skagerrak. The Vance et al. 2021 Figshare data could give this but has not been analysed for it.
- I found no published harbour seal dive study from the Skagerrak or Kattegat. Danish DCE and Norwegian IMR telemetry appears to be haul-out and movement focused.
- I could not open full texts of Ramasco 2015, Bjørge 1995, Tollit 1998, Thompson & Miller 1990, or Wilson et al. 2015 (Wadden Sea, 319,021 dives from 34 seals), so their diel results are unverified.
- No measurement of capture success (as distinct from effort) versus light level exists in the sources found.

## 2. Haul-out diel rhythm (ashore by day or night)

### Takeaway
The haul-out rhythm varies by region and season. In the Kattegat, the region closest to the model area, harbour seals are reported to haul out mostly at night all year, except adults in the mating season. That implies daytime at-sea activity, but it is a timing pattern, not evidence of light-limited hunting. In Orkney the daily rhythm is clear in summer and disappears in winter.

### Cited Findings
- [Kattegat and western Baltic; search snippet only, full text 403] Galatius et al. 2026 (Mar. Mamm. Sci., doi 10.1111/mms.70208) tagged 66 harbour seals and 34 grey seals in 2004-2017. "Kattegat harbor seals had highest haulout probability during the night throughout the year, except for adults shifting to daytime preference during the mating season." Not verified against the full text — [Galatius et al. 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mms.70208)
- [Orkney, Scotland; abstract] Thompson, Fedak, McConnell & Nicholas 1989 (J. Appl. Ecol. 26:521-535) used radio telemetry in 1984-86. In summer both sexes hauled out daily with a marked diurnal pattern, and circadian timing mattered more than the tide. In winter seals spent more time offshore and showed no marked diurnal haul-out pattern — [Thompson et al. 1989](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/seasonal-and-sex-related-variation-in-the-activity-patterns-of-co/); [BES record](https://www.britishecologicalsociety.org/applied-ecology-resources/document/19901425995/)
- [West Scotland; abstract] Cunningham et al. 2009 tagged 24 harbour seals by satellite. Time hauled out had daily means of 11-27 % and varied by place, time and sex — [Cunningham et al. 2009](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/harbour-seal-movements-and-haul-out-patterns-implications-for-mon/)
- [Svalbard; full text] Hamilton et al. 2014 found a diel haul-out rhythm in months with a day-night cycle or midnight sun, but none in the polar night. Haul-out probability peaked at midday in September, October and April. Seals "preferred to haul out in the darkest part of the day in February and March". Time hauled out fell from ~5.2 h/day in September to ~1.2 h/day in February, and seals showed a strong low-tide preference on land — [Hamilton et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3899210)
- [General review statement; snippet] Across populations, harbour seals spend most time hauled out in daytime and at low tide, and most foraging trips take place at night. This conflicts with the Kattegat (night haul-out) and Porsangerfjord (daylight foraging) findings — [NAMMCO harbour seal page](https://nammco.no/harbour-seal/)

### Inferences
- The regions disagree. Kattegat and Porsangerfjord point to daytime at-sea foraging, while the review generalisation and Svalbard in autumn point to night foraging. A single "seals are day hunters" or "night hunters" rule therefore has no support. The diel timing of trips responds to local prey, tide, disturbance and social factors.
- For the model, a learned rest/forage timing (the Rest action plus the light observation) is the right place for this signal, not `dark_ratio`. If the Kattegat pattern were put into `dark_ratio` as well, it would be counted twice.

### Gaps
- I did not get the full Galatius et al. 2026 text, so season-by-age detail and any Skagerrak-specific (Norwegian/Swedish coast) haul-out data are missing.
- I found no study pairing haul-out timing with measured at-sea foraging success in the Kattegat or Skagerrak.

## 3. Seasonal patterns (long winter nights vs summer)

### Takeaway
In winter, seals spend more time at sea (Orkney, Svalbard) and dive deeper, longer and more pelagically (Svalbard), and diel structure tends to fade. Seasonal changes are driven by prey switching (codfish in autumn, pelagic fish in winter in N Norway) more than by the length of the day.

### Cited Findings
- [Svalbard; full text] Winter and early spring dives were "deeper (~150 m), longer (~480 sec), less numerous (~250 dives/day)" and "more pelagic", with less bottom time than in early autumn. The autumn diel pattern "disappeared during the Polar Night and did not reappear during spring or early summer". The authors infer that seals "adjust to their prey's change in behaviour", because cod and polar cod stop migrating vertically in the polar night — [Blanchet et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0132686)
- [Svalbard; full text] Time hauled out fell to ~1.2 h/day in February, and mean at-sea periods peaked at ~1.6 days in February — [Hamilton et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3899210)
- [Orkney; abstract] In winter seals spent more time offshore with no marked diurnal haul-out rhythm — [Thompson et al. 1989](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/seasonal-and-sex-related-variation-in-the-activity-patterns-of-co/)
- [N Norway, Porsangerfjord; snippet] Small codfish were preferred in autumn. When they moved into cold deep water to overwinter, seals switched to small pelagic fish — [Ramasco 2015](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/8149)
- [Salish Sea; full text] Bout-type use differed between seasons, and the night shift to shallow bouts was seen in the breeding season — [Wilson et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3981695/)

### Inferences
- Long winter nights do not appear to cut foraging. Seals spend more time at sea in winter, which would be the opposite of what a strong darkness penalty predicts. This is indirect, because time at sea is not success.
- Svalbard is high-Arctic with polar night and is not a direct analogue for 58 N. It is still the clearest evidence that seals keep foraging, more pelagically, through near-continuous darkness.

### Gaps
- I found no seasonal diel dive data for North Sea, Skagerrak or Kattegat harbour seals.
- I found no data on winter foraging success (as opposed to effort) for any population.

## 4. Grey seal fallback

### Takeaway
Grey seals show the same picture. Pelagic diving increases at night (SW Ireland, NW Atlantic, Baltic juveniles), consistent with prey rising in the water column. Baltic grey seals haul out at night and make benthic feeding dives by day. Light shapes the type and timing of foraging, but no grey seal study I found shows that darkness suppresses foraging overall.

### Cited Findings
- [SW Ireland, Blasket Islands; full text] Jessopp et al. 2013 (PLOS ONE) tracked 8 females (324,900 dives; tags deployed February 2009 for ~7-8 months). "Overall, pelagic dives were more frequent at night (44 % ± 24 %) than day (28 % ± 17 %, F7,34 = 11.41, P = 0.002)." The night excess held over all sediment types: fine 52 % vs 36 %, coarse 39 % vs 28 %, rock 39 % vs 20 %. 69 % of dives in water > 50 m were benthic. The authors read this as "a response to the diurnal migration of prey up into the water column at night", with light influencing foraging "probably indirectly" — [Jessopp et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3646810/)
- [Baltic Sea; full text] Sjöberg, McConnell & Fedak 1999 (Wildlife Biology 5:37-47) found that "peak numbers of grey seals in the Baltic Sea hauled out at night". Satellite-tagged seals (n = 11) were most often hauled out 21:00-02:00 GMT, with haul-outs starting near sunset and ending near sunrise. They cite Sjöberg et al. 1995: "benthic feeding dives occurred during daytime". They attribute the nocturnal haul-out to prey (herring) behaviour, not predator avoidance — [Sjöberg et al. 1999](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/wildlife-biology/volume-5/issue-1/wlb.1999.007/Haulout-patterns-of-grey-seals-iHalichoerus-grypus-i-in-the/10.2981/wlb.1999.007.full)
- [Baltic, single sub-adult; snippet] Sjöberg et al. 1995 tracked one seal for 115 days. Square "foraging" dives were more common around midday, and haul-outs at night — [St Andrews portal](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/movements-and-diurnal-behaviour-patterns-in-a-baltic-grey-seal-em/)
- [Baltic, rehabilitated juveniles; search snippet, preprint] Day dives were generally deeper and shorter, and night dives shallower and longer, which the authors link to prey rising at night — [Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9142555/v1)
- [NW Atlantic young-of-year; search snippet] Benthic diving peaked in daylight, and pelagic diving was more frequent at night — [Animal Biotelemetry 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40317-025-00405-5)
- [North Sea; snippet] McConnell et al. 1999 tracked grey seals from the Farne Islands and Abertay by satellite. Trips to offshore sandy or gravel areas were judged to be foraging, but "we have no direct evidence of when feeding was taking place" — [McConnell et al. 1999](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1046/j.1365-2664.1999.00429.x)
- [Britain, grey and harbour seals; abstract] Russell et al. 2015 (Oikos 124:1462-1472) studied 63 grey and 126 harbour seals and included time of day as a covariate in activity-budget models. The two species were temporally separated in time hauled out but not in time foraging. I could not access the time-of-day effect itself — [Russell et al. 2015](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/8329)

### Inferences
- The grey seal evidence repeats the harbour seal picture. Night shifts foraging toward the water column (pelagic prey rising), and benthic feeding takes place by day and, in Jessopp's data, at night as well (56 % of night dives were benthic). The Baltic pattern of benthic feeding by day and haul-out at night is the strongest hint of daytime-biased benthic foraging. The authors explain it by prey behaviour, not by visual limits.

### Gaps
- The time-of-day results in Russell et al. 2015, the most relevant UK dataset with both species, were not accessible (PDF download timed out).
- I found no grey seal data from the Skagerrak or Kattegat.

## 5. Synthesis for the model: does darkness limit seal foraging success?

### Takeaway
The field evidence does not support `dark_ratio` < 1 for seals on either prey. No study I found shows lower capture success in darkness. Diel patterns, where present, are explained by prey movement, and on pelagic prey they show seals foraging as much or more at night. Recommendation: `dark_ratio` = 1 (no light effect) on both seals -> `gadoids` and seals -> `pelagic_fish`. Diel timing is left to the learned Rest/forage policy, which already observes the light level.

### Cited Findings
- Night is associated with more or deeper dives in harbour seals (Svalbard autumn; Monterey dive counts), and with more pelagic dives in grey seals (SW Ireland 44 % vs 28 %). Both are interpreted as prey rising at night — [Blanchet et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0132686); [Jessopp et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3646810/); [ATOC record](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftdtic:ADA330718)
- Benthic-feeding harbour seals (Norway, Moray Firth) show "little diurnal variation in dive depths or types" — [secondary citation in Wilson et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3981695/)
- Day-biased activity is reported in the Kattegat (night haul-out) and Porsangerfjord (daylight foraging), and for Baltic grey seals (benthic dives by day, haul-out at night). The latter is explained by prey behaviour — [Galatius et al. 2026 (snippet)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mms.70208); [Ramasco 2015 (snippet)](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/8149); [Sjöberg et al. 1999](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/wildlife-biology/volume-5/issue-1/wlb.1999.007/Haulout-patterns-of-grey-seals-iHalichoerus-grypus-i-in-the/10.2981/wlb.1999.007.full)
- Harbour seals can track fish wakes with their vibrissae, a sense that does not depend on light — [Dehnhardt et al. 2001 via JEB follow-up](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article/210/5/781/17300/Tracking-of-biogenic-hydrodynamic-trails-in)

### Inferences
- **seals -> gadoids (~95 % of diet; demersal fish and flatfish):** set `dark_ratio` = 1. Prey sit on or near the bottom at 20-100 m, which is dim at any sun elevation. Seals hunt them close to the sea bed with a tactile and hydrodynamic sense, and the benthic-feeding populations show little diel variation. Nothing measured supports a surface-light-driven success penalty. `light_threshold_deg` is then irrelevant.
- **seals -> pelagic_fish (~5 %; herring/sprat):** the evidence (Jessopp; Svalbard autumn; Baltic juveniles) suggests seals do as well or better at night, when pelagic prey rise. The model cannot represent `dark_ratio` > 1, so the most faithful allowed value is also 1. Night-time accessibility of herring is already partly represented on the prey side, since herring -> zooplankton carries its own daylight term.
- **Day-active pattern in the Kattegat or Baltic:** this is a timing signal (when seals are at sea), not a per-attempt success signal. The model's Rest action plus the light observation lets the policy learn it if it pays. Putting the same pattern into `dark_ratio` would count it twice.
- **Fallback, if a mild day bias is wanted anyway:** a gadoid `dark_ratio` around 0.8 could be justified only as a proxy for the Kattegat day-active pattern. That is a modelling choice, not a literature value, and it is weaker than leaving the timing to the policy. The cited literature does not give a numerical darkness-to-daylight success ratio for seals.
- Confidence: moderate. Rejecting a light penalty rests on the absence of positive evidence for it across about 10 studies and on mechanism (bottom depth, vibrissae), not on a direct day/night capture-success measurement.

### Gaps
- No study gives a direct day/night comparison of capture success (prey-capture attempts per unit foraging time, or success per attempt) for North Sea or Skagerrak harbour seals. The closest route is to reanalyse the Vance et al. 2021 Figshare data (timestamped prey-capture attempts, Wadden Sea).
- No Skagerrak-specific dive telemetry was found, and the Galatius et al. 2026 Kattegat haul-out result is a snippet, not verified against the full text.
- The value of `light_threshold_deg` for seals cannot be constrained from the literature found. It does not matter if `dark_ratio` = 1.
