# Evidence: predator odour, plume physics and predator-prey overlap

Per source: what was measured, the numbers, the grade, and where it was read.
Grades: [F] field data, [L] lab data, [J] judgement/inference, [A] abstract or
secondary source only. "Read:" says what was actually opened. Downloads are in
`data/` (gitignored). Compiled 2026-11-10. No value here was derived from model
output.

Unless stated, a number is for ONE species, not a biomass-weighted group value.
No source below gives a group-level (biomass-weighted) value for the model's
benthivores, whiting or planktivores.

## A. Detection of predator cues by fish (question 1)

### E1. Jutfelt F, Hedgarde M (2013). Atlantic cod actively avoid CO2 and predator odour, even after long-term CO2 exposure. Frontiers in Zoology 10: 81. doi:10.1186/1742-9994-10-81
- Measured: choice of juvenile Atlantic cod between two laminar water masses, one
  carrying predator odour. Kristineberg (Gullmarsfjord, Skagerrak coast).
- Numbers: cod 9.4 +- 1.4 cm, 8.3 g. Two-choice flume (Loligo), arena 32 x 40 cm,
  water 15 cm deep, laminar flow 1 cm/s, < 1 cm mixed zone between the masses. Each
  side fed 17 L/min from a 200 L header tank; the cue tank held one live wild-caught
  500 g brown trout (Salmo trutta). Both control and CO2-exposed cod avoided the
  predator side (Wilcoxon p < 0.0001 each; n 19 / 12); no difference between groups.
- Scale: centimetres, undiluted tank water. Says nothing about range, threshold or
  density. Predator is a coastal piscivore, not a gadoid.
- Grade: [L]. Only lab demonstration found of a gadoid avoiding predator odour.
- Read: full text (Europe PMC XML, data/jutfelt2013.txt).

### E2. Utne ACW, Bacchi B (1997). The influence of visual and chemical stimuli from cod Gadus morhua on the distribution of two-spotted goby Gobiusculus flavescens. Sarsia 82: 129-135. doi:10.1080/00364827.1997.10413646
- Measured: distribution of 10 gobies between two feeders when a cod (0-group,
  15-17 cm) was presented by image, odour or both at one feeder. Arena 0.32 m2.
- Result: gobies followed the ideal free distribution without a predator, avoided
  the cod feeder with image and/or odour; avoidance persisted 1-2 days; image had
  the strongest immediate effect, odour the stronger persistence. Odour of an
  injured conspecific (alarm substance) had NO significant effect.
- Grade: [L], marine, cod as the predator. Metre scale.
- Read: full text PDF (data/utne_bacchi1997.pdf).

### E3. Utne-Palm AC (2001). Response of naive two-spotted gobies Gobiusculus flavescens to visual and chemical stimuli of their natural predator, cod Gadus morhua. Marine Ecology Progress Series 218: 267-274. doi:10.3354/meps218267
- Result: naive gobies avoided a habitat with a VISUAL cod; cod ODOUR alone had no
  effect, also the day after one session with a live cod. After a live cod on 3
  consecutive feeding sessions the gobies avoided cod odour, still significant
  6 months later. "Innate fear of any larger fish, while the recognition of a
  specific predator odour has to be learned."
- Grade: [L], marine. Implication [J]: predator odour is a learned label paired with
  other evidence (sight, attacks), not an innate density meter.
- Read: full text PDF (data/utnepalm2001.pdf).

### E4. Atkinson CJL, Bergmann M, Kaiser MJ (2004). Habitat selection in whiting. Journal of Fish Biology 64: 788-793. doi:10.1111/j.1095-8649.2004.00340.x
- Result (abstract): lab whiting preferred sand > gravel > emergent structure; a
  "predator stimulus" increased the sand preference of large whiting and the
  structure preference of small whiting. A search snippet describes the stimulus as
  a shadow (visual); not verified.
- Grade: [A]. The only whiting antipredator study found; tank scale; not olfactory
  as far as the abstract shows.
- Read: Crossref abstract.

### E5. Lokkeborg S (1998). Feeding behaviour of cod, Gadus morhua: activity rhythm and chemically mediated food search. Animal Behaviour 56: 371-378. doi:10.1006/anbe.1998.0772
- Measured: 8 acoustically tagged cod in a north-Norwegian fjord, stationary
  positioning system, 3-8 days, with baited lines.
- Result (abstract as quoted by indexes): cod detected a FOOD odour source "from a
  distance of several hundred metres" and made a rheotactic (up-current) search;
  fish inside the plume located baits more often than fish outside it. The 2014
  review (E7) cites the same study as attraction from 700 m, baits located 7 h after
  setting.
- Grade: [F] study, but [A] for the numbers (full text not opened; 700 m is a
  secondary citation). Food odour, not predator odour; detection only downstream.
- Read: abstract fragments via agris/openpolar search results.

### E6. Lokkeborg S, Bjordal A, Ferno A (1989). Responses of cod (Gadus morhua) and haddock (Melanogrammus aeglefinus) to baited hooks in the natural environment. Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences 46: 1478-1483. doi:10.1139/f89-189
- Result (abstract): underwater TV; both species swam UPSTREAM to baited hooks;
  activity decreased at high current velocities; haddock responded more often but
  less intensely.
- Grade: [F] (abstract read). Haddock and cod use odour plumes, current-directional.
- Read: Crossref abstract.

### E7. Lokkeborg S, Siikavuopio SI, Humborstad O-B, Utne-Palm AC, Ferter K (2014). Towards more efficient longline fisheries: fish feeding behaviour, bait characteristics and development of alternative baits. Reviews in Fish Biology and Fisheries 24: 985-1003. doi:10.1007/s11160-014-9360-z
- Claims (secondary snippets): the bait plume is the main driver of attraction; fish
  swim up-current; cod 700 m (Lokkeborg 1998); bait odours last many hours; cod
  located baits ~50 % faster by day; food-deprived fish respond to lower odour
  concentrations than satiated fish.
- Grade: [A]. Open access but the publisher PDF was not retrievable here.
- Read: search-engine snippets only.

### E8. Stoner AW (2004). Effects of environmental variables on fish feeding ecology: implications for the performance of baited fishing gear and stock assessment. Journal of Fish Biology 65: 1445-1471. doi:10.1111/j.0022-1112.2004.00593.x
- Claim (abstract): the active space of a bait depends on environment and behaviour;
  temperature, light, current velocity and prey density may change catchability
  ~10-fold.
- Grade: [A]. Read: Crossref abstract.

### E9. Wisenden BD, Rugg ML, Korpi NL, Fuselier LC (2009). Lab and field estimates of active time of chemical alarm cues of a cyprinid fish and an amphipod crustacean. Behaviour 146: 1423-1442. doi:10.1163/156853909X440998
- Measured: responses of fathead minnows to skin extract aged 0, 3, 6 h at 18 C, lab
  and lake trap field test (FRESHWATER).
- Numbers: response to fresh and 3 h cue, none at 6 h; active time 3-6 h. Cited
  within: lab dilution series, 1 cm2 minnow skin activates 58 000 L, a sphere of
  radius 2.3 m (Lawrence & Smith 1989); natural minnow and dace populations avoid a
  radius of 2-8 m around 2 cm2 of skin (Wisenden 2008, Behaviour 145: 391-407,
  doi:10.1163/156853908783402920; not opened). "Advection ... exerts an overriding
  influence on both active space and active time. Even in wind-sheltered lakes ...
  advection dilutes cues below the threshold of sensitivity long before 2 h."
  Other active times cited: woodfrog alarm cue 5 min-4 h (field), tadpole cues
  48-72 h (lab), snail response to sunfish diet cue half-life 41 h, crayfish to
  turtle odour 1 h but not 2 h.
- Grade: [L]+[F], freshwater. Read: full text PDF (data/wisenden2009.pdf).

### E10. Chivers DP, Dixson DL, White JR, McCormick MI, Ferrari MCO (2013). Degradation of chemical alarm cues and assessment of risk throughout the day. Ecology and Evolution 3: 3925-3934. doi:10.1002/ece3.760
- Measured: avoidance by juvenile damselfish (Pomacentrus ambonensis) of alarm cues
  aged in natural ocean water (tropical reef).
- Numbers: fresh cue -> strong avoidance; aged 10-20 min -> intermediate; aged
  30 min -> no response; degradation fastest early-mid afternoon (UV, temperature,
  O2, pH high).
- Grade: [F] (in situ ocean water), MARINE but tropical; alarm cue, not kairomone.
- Read: Europe PMC abstract.

### E11. Ferrari MCO, Messier F, Chivers DP (2006). The nose knows: minnows determine predator proximity and density through detection of predator odours. Animal Behaviour 72: 927-932. doi:10.1016/j.anbehav.2006.03.001
- Claim (secondary): fathead minnows grade their response to pike odour by cue
  concentration (proximity) and by the number of pike (relative density) - "the first
  to show" this. Exact design and numbers not verified (the snippet was garbled).
- Grade: [A], [L], FRESHWATER, single tank (well-mixed water, no plume).
- Read: search snippets only.

### E12. Pfeiffer W (1977). The distribution of fright reaction and alarm substance cells in fishes. Copeia 1977(4): 653-665. doi:10.2307/1443164
- Claim (secondary): alarm-substance cells and the fright reaction are typical of
  Ostariophysi; rare elsewhere. Utne & Bacchi (E2) summarise Pfeiffer: fish with the
  fright reaction are mostly social, lack defensive structures and are
  non-predaceous.
- No source found showing a Schreckstoff/alarm-cue system in gadoids or clupeids
  (searches for herring and cod skin-extract responses returned nothing; E2 found
  no goby response to conspecific extract). Gap, not evidence of absence.
- Grade: [A]. Read: citing texts.

### E13. Wagner CM, Bals JD, Hanson ME, Scott AM (2022). Attenuation and recovery of an avoidance response to a chemical antipredator cue in an invasive fish. Conservation Physiology 10: coac019. doi:10.1093/conphys/coac019
- Numbers: sea lamprey, continuous alarm cue -> reversible attenuation over 2-4 h,
  recovery 30-60 min after removal; sporadic volitional encounters over 5 h -> no
  attenuation; olfactory neurons adapt quickly and recover within 15 min (EOG).
- Grade: [L], jawless, freshwater-phase animals. Read: Europe PMC abstract.

### E14. Feder ME, Wagner CM (2025). Building a natural repellent: effects of varying alarm cue exposure on swim activity and spatial avoidance in an invasive fish. Conservation Physiology 13: coaf028. doi:10.1093/conphys/coaf028
- Result: constant concentration -> activity response declines; on/off partly and
  varying concentration fully prevent the decline; spatial-avoidance habituation not
  seen in their small arena ("test systems ... should be carefully matched to the
  scale of the behavioural response").
- Grade: [L]. Read: Europe PMC abstract.

### E14b. Achtymichuk GH, Fish KM, Ferrari MCO (2025). The power of concentration: antipredator responses to diluted frozen crayfish alarm cues ... PLoS ONE 20: e0340001. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0340001
- Claim (abstract): animals with threat sensitivity "typically have graded responses
  to increasing concentrations of these cues which plateau when a maximal response is
  reached"; most alarm-cue studies use one crushed animal in 100-400 mL, which may be
  above ecologically relevant concentrations.
- Grade: [A], crayfish (context only, not fish). Read: Europe PMC abstract (truncated).

### E15. Vail AL, McCormick MI (2011). Metamorphosing reef fishes avoid predator scent when choosing a home. Biology Letters 7: 921-924. doi:10.1098/rsbl.2011.0380
- Result: naturally settling damselfish settled 24-43 % less to habitat patches
  emitting predator odour than to control patches (field experiment).
- Grade: [F], marine tropical, patch scale (metres; patch spacing not verified).
- Read: Europe PMC abstract.

## B. Plume physics (question 2)

### E16. Webster DR, Weissburg MJ (2001). Chemosensory guidance cues in a turbulent chemical odor plume. Limnology and Oceanography 46: 1034-1047. doi:10.4319/lo.2001.46.5.1034
- Measured: dye plumes (PLIF) in a turbulent boundary layer of a large flume.
- Quoted results: "the time-averaged concentration converges far too slowly to be
  useful to a foraging animal"; "only mild variation with distance from the source is
  observed in the concentration burst magnitude and duration"; cues depend on release
  height, velocity and nozzle size as well as ambient flow.
- Grade: [L]. Read: Crossref abstract.

### E17. Finelli CM, Pentcheff ND, Zimmer-Faust RK, Wethey DS (1999). Odor transport in turbulent flows: constraints on animal navigation. Limnology and Oceanography 44: 1056-1071. doi:10.4319/lo.1999.44.4.1056
- Result (abstract): field plume measurements; plumes are "discrete odor filaments
  separated by clean water"; the time needed to build a time-averaged profile is
  much longer than neural/behavioural time scales.
- Grade: [F] (coastal, metre scale). Read: Crossref abstract.

### E18. Moore P, Crimaldi J (2004). Odor landscapes and animal behavior: tracking odor plumes in different physical worlds. Journal of Marine Systems 49: 55-64. doi:10.1016/j.jmarsys.2003.05.005
- Content: near the bed, chemicals are streaky and persistent; above it, rapid
  turbulent stirring and "high levels of intermittency (patchiness)"; at large scales
  the odour field "is chaotic ... changes occur rapidly and unpredictably". Km-scale
  chemical orientation is cited for salmon (large DIFFUSE river-water source in
  stratified layers) and for sharks (point source, zigzag up-current).
- Grade: review ([J] on physics, lab/field citations). Read: full text PDF
  (data/moore_crimaldi2004.pdf). DOI checked on Crossref.

### E19. Ikawa T, Okubo A, Okabe H, Cheng L (1998). Oceanic diffusion and the pelagic insects Halobates spp. Marine Biology 131: 195-201. (Builds on Okubo A 1971, Oceanic diffusion diagrams, Deep-Sea Research 18: 789-802, not opened.)
- Content: from Okubo's diagrams (dye/float data, 1 h-1 month, 100 m-100 km),
  horizontal patch variance sigma_rc^2 ~ 5.15e-5 t^3 (cm2, t in s); apparent
  diffusivity K ~ l^(4/3).
- Arithmetic [J]: t = 6 h (21 600 s) gives sigma_rc ~ 230 m, patch radius
  1.5 sigma ~ 340 m; t = 1 h gives sigma ~ 15 m. Open-ocean SURFACE relation; the
  stratified near-bed layer of the Skagerrak may differ.
- Grade: [A] for Okubo, [J] for the arithmetic. Read: full text PDF (data/
  escholarship_qt1fp9644f.pdf).

## C. Behaviour and spatial overlap (question 3)

### E20. Rose GA, Leggett WC (1990). The importance of scale to predator-prey spatial correlations: an example of Atlantic fishes. Ecology 71: 33-43. doi:10.2307/1940245
- Measured: acoustic transects of cod and capelin, Newfoundland, 1985-86; spectral
  coherence by scale.
- Numbers (abstract): coherent over 2-30 km when capelin were not in thermal refuges;
  in phase (positive) at > 4-10 km; out of phase (negative) below the aggregation
  dimension (3.5 km), maximum negative correlation at 2-3 km in both years; with
  capelin in thermal refuges, in phase only at scales larger than the refuges; when
  cod were actually feeding, in phase with capelin at scales as small as 3.5 m.
- Grade: [F] (abstract read). Pelagic prey, gadoid predator.
- Read: Crossref abstract.

### E21. Horne JK (1994). Spatial variance of capelin (Mallotus villosus) in coastal Newfoundland waters. Journal of Northwest Atlantic Fishery Science 17: 33-43.
- Content: capelin spatial variance episodic; capelin-predator coherence "predicted
  to concentrate at large spatial scales" over a season, "at the scale of a foraging
  bout (minutes to hours) ... episodically concentrated at any single spatial scale".
- Grade: [F] for capelin variance, [J] for the predator prediction.
- Read: full text PDF (data/horne_nafo1994.pdf).

### E22. Kempf A, Floeter J, Temming A (2008). Predator-prey overlap induced Holling type III functional response in the North Sea fish assemblage. Marine Ecology Progress Series 367: 295-308. doi:10.3354/meps07555
- Measured: ICES stomach data (1981, 1985-87, 1991), predators cod 30-50 cm and
  whiting 24-40 cm, prey including cod, whiting and haddock 5-20 cm; Schoener overlap
  per ICES rectangle (30 x 30 n.mi., ~55 km) from IYFS/IBTS/EGFS.
- Result: prey composition and overlap explain 65.6 % of diet deviance; a prey
  refuge at low prey abundance from switching AND from falling overlap as the prey's
  range contracts; diet share drops fast when overlap < ~0.1.
- Grade: [F]. Scale is ~55 km, three orders above the model's cell.
- Read: full text PDF (data/kempf2008.pdf).

### E23. Trenkel VM, Pinnegar JK, Dawson WA, du Buit MH, Tidd AN (2005). Spatial and temporal structure of predator-prey relationships in the Celtic Sea fish community. Marine Ecology Progress Series 299: 257-268. doi:10.3354/meps299257
- Measured: haul-level survey densities and stomachs, 1982-95; cod > 50, hake,
  megrim, whiting > 20 cm as predators.
- Result: "general independence between predator and prey distributions"; whiting's
  distribution "closely matched that of a key prey, Trisopterus spp." (local
  collocation index 0.51 spring, 0.59 autumn; cod-Trisopterus 0.29-0.32), read as an
  aggregative response.
- Grade: [F]. Read: full text PDF (data/trenkel2005.pdf). DOI checked on Crossref.

### E24. Temming A, Floeter J, Ehrich S (2007). Predation hot spots: large scale impact of local aggregations. Ecosystems 10: 865-876. doi:10.1007/s10021-007-9066-3
- Claim (secondary): survey distributions dominated by a few very high catches;
  stomachs showed predators (cod, whiting listed) aggregating on prey aggregations;
  local hot spots have system-wide effects.
- Grade: [A]. Read: index snippets.

### E25. Hammond JI, Luttbeg B, Sih A (2007). Predator and prey space use: dragonflies and tadpoles in an interactive game. Ecology 88: 1525-1535. doi:10.1890/06-1236. With Sih A (1998, 2005), "leapfrog" game models.
- Claim (secondary): with both present, predators preferred the prey's
  high-resource patch and large prey shifted to a uniform distribution; prey
  avoidance mattered more than predator attraction. Game models predict predators
  match the prey's RESOURCES; the effect weakens with risk to the intermediate
  predator from top predators.
- Grade: [A], [L] freshwater. Read: snippets.

### E26. Lima SL, Bednekoff PA (1999). Temporal variation in danger drives antipredator behavior: the predation risk allocation hypothesis. American Naturalist 153: 649-659. doi:10.1086/303202
- Content (via Fernandez-Juricic & Rodriguez-Prieto 2010, Encyclopedia of Animal
  Behavior 3: 75-78, read): when high risk is brief and infrequent prey invest most
  in it; when frequent or lengthy, prey must cut antipredator effort in each risky
  situation to meet energy needs; prey in areas of high predator abundance are
  predicted to be LESS wary. Tests mixed; cichlid fish support; North Pacific
  juvenile flatfishes differ by species (Boersma et al. 2008, Behav Ecol Sociobiol 62:
  1959-1968, doi:10.1007/s00265-008-0627-z, not opened). Habituation and risk
  allocation are hard to tell apart.
- Grade: [J] theory, [A] for tests. Read: encyclopedia PDF
  (data/ferrari_encyclopedia_riskalloc.pdf - authors are Fernandez-Juricic and
  Rodriguez-Prieto despite the file name).

### E27. Helfman GS (1989). Threat-sensitive predator avoidance in damselfish-trumpetfish interactions. Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology 24: 47-58. doi:10.1007/BF00300117
- Claim (abstract snippet): 11 threespot damselfish (29-114 mm) vs trumpetfish models
  on a rod; avoidance graded by distance, model size and strike posture; habituation
  more common to weak threats; smaller damselfish more sensitive.
- Grade: [A], [F] (in situ), VISUAL, metre scale. Read: snippets.

### E28. Linehan JE, Gregory RS, Schneider DC (2001). Predation risk of age-0 cod (Gadus) relative to depth and substrate in coastal waters. Journal of Experimental Marine Biology and Ecology 263: 25-44.
- Claim (secondary): juvenile cod segregate from adult cod by depth/substrate in
  nursery areas, thought to reduce cannibalism. Habitat-scale (depth class) not
  within-habitat avoidance.
- Grade: [A]. Read: citing snippets only.

### E29. Project field analysis (not literature): research_notes/"Vitlingens uteblivna predation", resume 266.7.
- GOV hauls in the box, 2000-20, fixed haul key: Lloyd's L whiting x cod >= 20 cm
  within bed-depth classes 1.33 (Q1) / 1.80 (Q3); x benthivore guild 1.55 / 1.77.
- Grade: [F] (project's own survey analysis; haul = a few km of tow, so this is a
  haul-scale co-occurrence, not a 1-km cell value).
